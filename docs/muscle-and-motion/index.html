When the view turns

The same person balances on a beam, first standing, then walking. The view through their headset turns 20° counterclockwise. Where does a marker on their lower back go, and what voltages do the electrodes on each leg pick up?

Paired sample clock

Each tick advances both recordings by 1/256 second. The cue falls between samples. The two clocks meet at the first sample after each cue, so their exact times since the logged rotation differ slightly. The cutoff follows 32 samples later.

Eight leads on the lower legs

Skin electrodes picked up voltage over four named muscles on each leg. Choose a lead to compare its signed waveform in the two recordings. A microvolt (µV) is one millionth of a volt.

Voltage amplitude across the cutoff

Squaring the voltages, averaging them and taking the square root gives one amplitude per interval (RMS). Compare the last 32 samples through the cutoff with the next 32. Both intervals span 125 ms. Voltage alone cannot give muscle force.

What the instruments saw

Steven M. Peterson and Daniel P. Ferris recorded the leg voltages at 1,000 Hz and the optical marker at 100 Hz. They synchronized and resampled both onto the stored 256 Hz clock. A logged command gives the rotation time. The timing of light at the eye, muscle force and the cause of a movement remain unknown. Data paper · Original experiment

These are two events from one person. Standing came before walking. This comparison cannot separate posture from session order or describe a typical response. The published channel names tell us which side and muscle were recorded. Exact electrode positions on these legs are unavailable. The standing recording lacks usable head motion. Both paths here follow only the sacrum.
