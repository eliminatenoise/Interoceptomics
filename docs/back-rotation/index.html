How far does the back sensor turn?

One person walks for five seconds, then stands still. A sensor strapped to their back records each small turn. Two patches on their skin pick up electrical activity at the same time.

What the skin picked up

Each dot on the patch is a metal contact. Its voltage is measured relative to a separate contact on the right shoulder blade.

Nearby muscles, the heartbeat and movement at the skin can all contribute. The voltage at one contact cannot tell us which nerve fired or which joint moved.

How the measurements and anatomy were made

The sensor stored four numbers for each orientation, called a quaternion. The authors list the components as W, X, Y and Z. We divide by their length, then compare the readings exactly 200 stored samples apart. Twice the inverse cosine of the absolute dot product gives the amount of turn, in radians. We convert it to degrees. Flipping the signs of all four numbers leaves that amount unchanged.

In the 3D sensor view, the block’s first orientation sets the reference. The camera shows the sensor in its own coordinate system. The recording gives no path through the room and no angles for individual vertebrae. The readings share a 2,000 Hz clock with the skin recordings, but the orientation sensor updates internally at 100 Hz. The viewer reads its orientation every 0.01 s.

The anatomical model shows the bones on either side of each landmark. Contact spacing, numbering and reference levels come from Experiment 2. The paper’s Figure 1 shows the actual recording arrangement. The study authors call the upper patch the brachial matrix and the lower patch the lumbar matrix. Those names describe the recording regions.

The skin plots average 20 consecutive raw voltage samples into each 0.01 s bin. The mean keeps its sign. Original null and noise flags are shown when they apply to the chosen contact. The display retains flagged readings, including any uncorrected artefacts. The four-person comparison describes these recordings only. Both graphs replay measurements.
