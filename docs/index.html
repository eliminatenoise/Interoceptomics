The Human Genome Project failed. Not at sequencing: it delivered the human reference sequence in 2003. It failed at what was promised next, and most rare diseases still have no approved treatment. A list of the parts is not an account of what they do. A wiring diagram of the nervous system has the same limit.

Interoceptomics is a proposed field of science that starts from the other end. Interoception is the body sensing its own interior: a heartbeat, a breath. Connectomics is the mapping of nerve connections. Put the two together and go function first: measure what the body senses and does, then ask which connections carry it. None of its experiments has been run yet.

Walk through the experiments, one step at a time

The experiment guide runs in your browser, with nothing to install. It follows one movement from a command to an outcome. Choose Small vision for the movement tasks in people, or Big vision for the six proposed animal stages and their 22 numbered steps. Each step has a 3D sketch you can turn, and each animal step links to the page of the technical script that describes it.

The Allen Institute's human brain atlas, as a 3D model you turn, cut and measure

A brain atlas is a published map that divides the brain into named regions. The viewer turns this one into a 3D model of the left half of the brain: 121 of the 141 regions the atlas draws there are in the model, and all 141 can be read in flat cuts through its grid of half-millimetre cubes. It colours the ones this project links to moving and feeling the body, to running its organs, or to both. It is still a map, not a measurement.

Open the Interoceptomics Atlas Viewer

It runs in your browser, with nothing to install: any current browser that can show 3D graphics (WebGL). The model is a 66 MB file, so the first view takes a moment. The viewer’s guide explains every control.

To use it offline, download it (ZIP, 40.5 MB) and extract the whole ZIP into a folder. Then open Windows.cmd on Windows or macOS.command on macOS, or run sh Linux.sh in a terminal on Linux; you need Python 3.10 or newer. The start file opens your browser on the viewer, at an address on your own computer; if the browser stays closed, copy the address the terminal shows into it. Keep its terminal window open while you explore, and press Ctrl+C in it to stop. The download installs nothing.

So far the viewer has been run on Linux, in Chromium. The Windows and macOS start files have not yet been tried on those systems, and the viewer has not been tried on a phone.

The viewer is an illustration built on a published atlas. It shows one averaged reference brain, left half. Its colours are this project's reading of published sources, not recorded activity: the viewer lists 54, and 49 of them are cited for at least one region. Nothing in it has been validated, scientifically or clinically.

The talk is paused; its scripts are published

In The Matrix, Neo has kung fu loaded into his brain through a cable. I Know Kung Fu is a talk of 111 slides that asks what a real version would have to measure, beginning with one movement. Slides 87 and 88 propose Interoceptomics as a field. The talk is paused. Its two scripts follow the same slides.

  • Technical script, 114 pages, every step argued in full with references: PDF · DOCX
  • Simple script, 36 pages, the essential spoken lines: PDF · DOCX

The slides are not published: 22 of their 88 pictures are known not to be cleared for redistribution, and the rights to the other 66 have not been confirmed. The scripts still describe those pictures. About the two scripts

The widest of three projects, and the least built

Interoceptomics belongs to Brain2Body, a programme about connecting brain and body function. NeuroTranslation asks how well one recording of the body predicts another, on open human recordings. NeuroSurge is a browser game for learning which motor areas of the brain move which parts of the body; its activity is simulated. Interoceptomics is the proposal that body sense and nerve wiring be studied together. So far it has a guide to its proposed experiments and two atlases to explore in the browser.
