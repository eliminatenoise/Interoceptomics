The Human Genome Project failed. Not at sequencing: it delivered the human reference sequence in 2003. It failed at what was promised next, and most rare diseases still have no approved treatment. A list of the parts is not an account of what they do. A wiring diagram of the nervous system has the same limit.

Interoceptomics is a proposed field of science that starts from the other end. Interoception is the body sensing its own interior: a heartbeat, a breath. Connectomics is the mapping of nerve connections. Put the two together and go function first: measure what the body senses and does, then ask which connections carry it. None of its experiments has been run yet.

Walk through the experiments, one step at a time

The experiment guide runs in your browser, with nothing to install. It follows one movement from a command to an outcome. Choose Small vision for the movement tasks in people, or Big vision for the six proposed animal stages and their 22 numbered steps. Each step has a 3D sketch you can turn, and each animal step links to the page of the technical script that describes it.

The Allen Institute's human brain atlas, as a 3D model you turn, cut and measure

A brain atlas is a published map that divides the brain into named regions. The viewer turns this one into a 3D model of the left half of the brain: all 141 regions the atlas draws there are in the model, twenty of them shown only when you ask, and all 141 can also be read in flat cuts through its grid of half-millimetre cubes. It colours the ones this project links to moving and feeling the body, to running its organs, or to both. It is still a map, not a measurement.

Open the Interoceptomics Atlas Viewer

It runs in your browser, with nothing to install: any current browser that can show 3D graphics (WebGL). The model is a 66 MB file, so the first view takes a moment. The viewer’s guide explains every control.

To use it offline, download it (ZIP, 64.3 MB) and extract the whole ZIP into a folder. Then open Windows.cmd on Windows or macOS.command on macOS, or run sh Linux.sh in a terminal on Linux; you need Python 3.10 or newer. The start file opens your browser on the viewer, at an address on your own computer; if the browser stays closed, copy the address the terminal shows into it. Keep its terminal window open while you explore, and press Ctrl+C in it to stop. The download installs nothing.

So far the viewer has been run on Linux, in Chromium. The Windows and macOS start files have not yet been tried on those systems, and the viewer has not been tried on a phone.

The viewer is an illustration built on a published atlas. It shows one averaged reference brain, left half. Its colours are this project's reading of published sources, not recorded activity: the viewer lists 54, and 49 of them are cited for at least one region. Nothing in it has been validated, scientifically or clinically.

Three projects, one question

Interoceptomics is one of three projects that ask the same question: what passes between the brain and the body? The brain sends orders out to the muscles, and the body reports back what it is doing and feeling. Each project comes at that traffic from a different side.

  • NeuroTranslation predicts one recording of the body from another, most of them of a movement: brain, nerve, muscle, force or motion, recorded by scientists who share their data openly. Every prediction is checked against what was actually recorded, and so far a signal’s own recent past, above all a hand’s own recent movement, has been the forecast to beat. Open its pages in your browser
  • NeuroSurge is a game for learning which part of the brain moves which part of the body. You look out from inside a body without skin. Turn your view to a coloured area of the brain in front of you, and the body part it controls moves; click a muscle and its area lights up. What the game’s brain does is made up by the game’s rules, not read from anyone’s brain. Play NeuroSurge in your browser
  • Interoceptomics, this project, is a proposed field of science that would measure what the body senses of its own interior, a heartbeat or a breath, and then ask which nerve connections carry it. None of its experiments has been run yet. The experiment guide walks through the proposed experiments one step at a time, each beside a 3D sketch you can turn. The atlas viewer is a 3D model of the left half of a human brain that you can turn, cut and measure. It is built from the Allen Institute’s published map of the brain’s regions: a map, not a measurement.

They are joined by a plan and a guide.

  • The game is meant to become the place where NeuroTranslation’s model is used. The plan: type or say an action, or move in front of your camera, and the body on screen does it, driven by NeuroTranslate 0.0, the one model NeuroTranslation is building to learn from many kinds of body signal at once. None of this is in the published game. A first draft is a proposed change to the game, not yet merged: commands you type or speak choose from a set of the game’s own movements, an arm or knee on screen follows yours as your camera sees it, and a map inside the game opens the recordings NeuroTranslation studies and its fitted models.
  • The experiment guide lays out the proposed experiments step by step, in two halves. Human movement (the “Small vision”) holds the movement tasks in people, where NeuroTranslation and the game meet; Animal learning (the “Big vision”) is Interoceptomics’ road map: six proposed stages of experiments in animals, in 22 numbered steps. Several of NeuroTranslation’s examples already sit inside the guide, among them two forecasts a quarter of a second ahead: the two angles of a person’s right wrist as they steer a cursor to a target, from three of its models, and where a rat on a W-shaped track will be.
