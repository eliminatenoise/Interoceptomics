Read the three measurements

A supported finger can stay still while glass slides past it. The apparatus measures normal force across the plate, perpendicular to the glass, and tangential force along it. In the original experiment, 4 N was the normal-force target. The displayed curve illustrates the magnitude of tangential force. A camera viewing the contact through the glass can track visible features on the skin. Changes in the relative positions of nearby features give local deformation. An electrode placed in the median nerve can record impulses from an individual sensory nerve fibre. Mapping its receptive field requires finding the patch of skin that affects its firing.

Delhaye, Jarocka, Barrea, Thonnard, Edin and Lefèvre (2021) studied passive tangential loading of a human right index or middle finger. They acquired surface force at 1 kHz, filmed the contact at 50 Hz and acquired neural signals at 12.8 kHz. The archived, discriminated spike-event vector is on a 1 kHz grid. Reproduce the constructed example

The count of six follows a check of one development trial. The selected source was the smallest single-volume optical ZIP accompanied by a matching protocol recording, first valid trial. Restrict the force samples and targets to the forward robot-motion interval common to zero- and one-based interpretations of the reported milliseconds. On the stored 1 kHz grid, each feature uses 20 consecutive samples. With the final sample at t, sum the binary spike indicators at t + 13 through t + 32 ms inclusive. Advance 20 samples per window. The 71 target windows contained six events. The count describes this portion of one trial.

In the reader the window number w runs from 1 to 71. For the invented mean load, let r be the smaller of 1 and (w − 1)/31. The displayed value is 0.30 + 1.50 × r² × (3 − 2r) newtons. The assigned spike windows are 7, 18, 27, 36, 53 and 65. Each has a count of one. All other windows have zero. The marks indicate the window in which a count occurs. Every displayed event position and load value is constructed, including their pairing. Playback slows the 20 ms steps to 130 ms so the transitions can be inspected. The plate travels an arbitrary graphical distance during this sequence.

In the separate skin drawing, horizontal width changes by the percentage on the slider. One vertex stays fixed while the two endpoints of the horizontal edge move. Width-based engineering strain is (current width − initial width)/initial width. The figure illustrates a single directional strain. The study also calculated spatially varying strains and strain rates. A recorded trace of strain at a particular receptive field would need a verified optical coordinate transform, the relevant saved columns and units, and a mapping from each camera exposure to the force and nerve-event grid. The two controls operate independently.

A companion NeuroTranslate experiment uses the same archive to forecast counts in three people held out from training. It counts events 21 through 40 ms after the last input sample. The illustration here uses the shorter 13–32 ms interval from the single development example. Read the three-person forecast results.

Compare the clocks

The camera clock control uses an invented spike at 44 ms and tracks an example frame at 40 ms plus the chosen offset. A 4 ms shift makes the frame and spike coincide on the example axis. The selected frame can lie on either side of the spike. The 50 Hz camera rate alone cannot choose an offset or identify the retained source frames. We inspected the first trials of recordings U115-01-trans1 and U104-02-trans1 from the Delhaye archive. Their source summaries each name 310 camera frames at 50 Hz. Their processed optical arrays each have 220 slices. Neither optical file identifies the frame indices or provides a clock mapping to the force and event grid. The chosen offsets and spike time in the demonstration are illustrative. Download the original recordings and metadata.

Check the macaque recording

A drum carried satin past the fingertip at 80 mm/s. The replay follows one rapidly adapting sensory fibre in the published macaque recordings. Times come from the start of the stored response. The archive supports a one-second counting interval at this speed. The instant of skin contact on the recording clock is unknown. Weber and colleagues’ original recording study · Delhaye and colleagues’ recording analysis

The Bensmaia Lab’s Feeling Fooled archive supplies the five recorded counts. We selected 100–200 ms from fibre 1, texture 1 (satin), repeat 1 in the second speed condition. Each window includes its start and ends just before the next boundary. The authors marked this repeat as recorded, and the count across the full stored second matches their deposited firing rate. We grouped the archived spike times into these five 20 ms windows. The derived counts retain the archive’s CC BY 4.0 licence. Original dataset · Included data licence · CC BY 4.0

To reproduce the counts, download Data_Files.zip from the original dataset. Run python check_recorded_bins.py Data_Files.zip with NumPy and SciPy installed. The script verifies the archive and counts the impulses in its periph/pda_neurons.mat member. Long, Lieber and Bensmaia used the same recording. Counting its archived spike times in their copy gives the same five values. Download the count check · Long and colleagues’ comparison

The forecast comes from a small transformer trained on spike histories from seven people. We took the first eligible forecast in order of fibre, texture, repeat and time. A forecast needed four earlier bins and a spike already seen since 100 ms. The model received only the four earlier counts. Its output had been saved before the next-bin counts were scored. This replay shows the first of two fixed fits (seed 431), rounded to two decimals. The other fit (seed 977) gave 0.23 expected spikes here. Across 81,476 eligible forecasts from 39 macaque fibres, a simple average combining recent counts with the human training rate scored better than either transformer. Compare the views with the original experiments

  • Delhaye and colleagues (2021), Figure 1A–C. The apparatus photograph shows the supported hand and a separate recording site at the forearm. The underside view and tracked triangles show how a global plate load differs from a local patch of skin. Figure 2 shows the measured sequence through contact, sliding and release. The reader isolates part of the loading phase. Its triangle uses geometric points and its contact shadow only helps locate the glass. Apparatus and contact tracking
  • Barrea, Delhaye, Lefèvre and Thonnard (2018), Figure 1A–C. Side and top views give the relative positions of finger, support and plate. The underside sequence follows the contact as the glass advances, with a 5 mm scale bar. The authors tested 2 N and 5 N normal loading. This was a separate perceptual experiment. Fingertip setup and successive contact views
  • Dzidek, Adams, Andrews, Zhang and Johnson (2017), Figure 1. A side photograph shows an inclined left index finger with its rounded pad against a flat platen. The 30° wedge is below the finger in that apparatus. Here the glass sits below the pad, as in the recordings. The mesh was oriented by its long axis and pad, then placed against the plate. The comparison supports the shape and orientation qualitatively. The model has arbitrary scale and a chosen pose. No individual contour, pressure distribution or contact footprint was fitted. Anatomy and software

The generic adult right index finger comes from the MakeHuman Team's artist-built CC0 hm08 surface. The source rig identifies the index finger as finger 2 on the model's right. Three pinned MakeHuman assets supply the surface, finger ownership weights and joints. The builder clips the proximal segment, smooths the mesh once, rotates its long axis down towards the glass and seats its lowest point on the plate. The skin colour, dorsal nail tint, glass and motion are illustrations. The dark shadow shows the contact side. The mesh carries no sensory nerve trajectory or receptive-field coordinate.
