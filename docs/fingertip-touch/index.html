When glass slips under a fingertip

Hold the finger still and move the glass. A force sensor reads the load across the whole contact. A camera sees the skin move in patches. An electrode in the median nerve listens to one sensory fibre.

What the camera adds

Pick three features on the skin and join them into a triangle. If two approach each other, that patch shortens along the line between them. A plate force in newtons can’t tell you how much shorter that line became.

Which camera frame came before the spike?

The archive gives force readings and spike times on a 1 ms grid. The camera recorded at 50 frames per second, one frame every 20 ms. Two inspected recordings each list 310 camera frames and contain 220 processed optical slices. The available files do not say where those slices land on the spike clock.

What was measured?

Delhaye and colleagues recorded electrical impulses from individual human sensory fibres during passive fingertip loading. In one development example, six spikes fell in 71 successive 20 ms counting windows. The force-and-firing illustration keeps that total. It uses a constructed load curve and places the six spikes in invented windows. Each mark represents a count within a window. It gives no exact firing time.

The load is the mean tangential force over 20 samples on a 1 kHz grid. Relative to the last load sample, the count covers samples 13 through 32 ms later. The camera in the experiment imaged the contact at 50 frames per second. Matching a patch in the archived optical files to a particular fibre and spike would require the patch location, strain-column definitions and a verified frame-to-event clock.
