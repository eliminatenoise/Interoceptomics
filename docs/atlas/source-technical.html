Where the anatomy comes from, and how to read it · Technical edition

The Interoceptomics Atlas Viewer draws a 3D model of the left half of the human brain from one published brain atlas, a map that divides the brain into named regions. Every surface, every cut and every number in the viewer comes from one file of that atlas: the grid of region numbers in the Allen Institute’s human reference atlas. This page states whose work the file is, what this project did to it, and how the viewer’s positions and measurements are defined. A surface is the skin that the viewer draws around a region, or around several regions merged together.

From that grid this project kept the labelled left half, drew surfaces around the regions, merged some regions and gave others a surface of their own, converted the coordinates, repacked the whole grid without loss for the cuts and the ruler, and added the colours, the cameras (the viewpoints from which the model is drawn) and the controls. Positions: millimetres on three axes

The atlas is a block of 394 × 466 × 378 cubes, each 0.5 mm on a side. Each cube (a voxel) carries the number of one region, or 0 for none. A position names the centre of a voxel with three numbers:

AxisGrows towardsFirst voxel centre
Rthe right of the head. Negative R is the left side. R = 0 is the midline, the boundary between the left and right halves of the head.−98 mm
Athe front of the head (anterior)−134 mm
Sthe top of the head (superior)−72 mm

Every position you type must sit on the grid: a multiple of 0.5 mm on each axis. The 3D model uses metres and another order of axes: it draws each point at (−R, S, A) ÷ 1000. The conversion is fixed, so a mark in the model and a mark in a cut are the same place.

A cut is a flat slice through the grid. On screen the three kinds of cut are laid out as follows:

The 3D model can be turned freely, so there the three letters at the corner of the picture show which way each axis points. R pointing straight towards you reads R ⊙, and straight away from you R ⊗.

The three flat cuts read the whole grid

Open three flat cuts shows three cuts through one voxel, straight from the grid, with nothing smoothed or filled in between voxels.

  • The colours only tell neighbouring regions apart, and a colour can repeat. They are not the colour groups of the 3D model.
  • The name and number under the crosshair (the mark where the three cuts cross) are the atlas’s own.
  • A dark tile (a tile is one voxel as a cut shows it) has no region.
  • The cuts show region numbers, not tissue. The atlas was drawn on an MRI picture (a scanner’s picture of the inside of the head), and that picture is not part of this viewer.

The grid labels almost only the left side of the brain. Of its 6,999,201 labelled voxels (voxels that belong to a region), 6,974,625 lie left of the midline, 14,152 on it and 10,424 right of it. The cuts show all of them as the atlas file holds them, with nothing mirrored. The 3D model draws the left side up to the midline. A teal cross marks your voxel in the 3D model when the voxel has a region and lies on that side.

ControlHow it is defined
Find a region, in the three flat cutsKnows the 141 regions that have voxels, on each side where they have them: 170 places in all. It lands on the voxel of the region that is nearest to the average position of all the region’s voxels on that side. A region that curves round its own middle has its average outside itself, and the finder still lands inside the region. If two voxels are equally near, it takes the one that comes first in the grid’s own order. The landing point is a place to start from, nothing more.
Volume of the selected regionCounts the voxels that carry exactly that number and multiplies by 0.125 mm³, the volume of one voxel. A voxel counts as left, midline or right by where its centre lies: R below 0, at 0, or above 0. A region and its sub-regions have separate numbers and are counted separately. A border one voxel wide is already 0.5 mm across. Borders follow the grid, so the volume is approximate.

The ruler is a straight line through the grid

ControlHow it is defined
Set A, Set BMark two voxel centres. The distance is the straight line between them, shown to three decimals although the grid itself steps in half millimetres.
B minus AThe same step, split along the three axes.
Follow the ruler through the cutsSplits the line wherever it crosses from one voxel into the next, reads the region number of each voxel it passes through from the whole grid, and joins neighbouring pieces that lie in the same region. A voxel that the line only grazes at an edge or a corner adds no length. A and B sit at voxel centres, so the first and last voxels contribute only the part of the line inside them. If the line leaves a region and comes back, the region gets two rows.

The line measures a distance in the atlas’s reference brain. It is not the route of a nerve fibre.

VPL and VPM, voxel by voxel

The ventral posterior lateral nucleus (VPL, region 10424) and the ventral posterior medial nucleus (VPM, region 10425) are two nuclei (clusters of nerve cells) that lie side by side in the thalamus, deep in the middle of the brain. Every voxel of both is on the left of the grid.

Show cuts through VPL and VPM reads a crop of the grid (a cut-out block), 34 × 27 × 30 voxels, that keeps these two regions and blanks everything else. Blue is VPL, copper with white dots is VPM, a dark line follows the edges they share in that layer, and grey is anything else, which may belong to another region. When the cuts first open, the coronal cut is at A = −20.5 mm, the sagittal cut at R = −12.5 mm and the axial cut at S = −2 mm. The crop.

The atlas names two divisions of VPL, rostral (a front part) and caudal (a back part), and one of VPM, parvocellular (a part made of small cells). It gives none of them voxels of its own, so the viewer cannot show them.

Published pictures of the same nuclei, for comparison by eye: Heckers et al., Figure 1 and Oishi et al., Figures 1–2: stained sections of the adult human thalamus (thin slices of tissue coloured so that their structure shows under a microscope), and MRI of brains after death. They are other brains, cut in other planes. No agreement with them has been measured.

The parietal operculum

The parietal operculum (PaO, region 13230) is the back part of the upper bank of the lateral sulcus, the deep groove along the side of the brain. It covers the back part of the insula (a stretch of the brain’s outer layer that lies folded deep inside the side of the brain) from above; further forward other regions do, chiefly the frontal operculum and the opercular part of the inferior frontal gyrus, both parts of a larger surface in the viewer. In the grid the parietal operculum has 43,319 voxels, 5,414.875 mm³, all on the left. Its surface in the model follows those voxels without smoothing. The surface file.

The atlas draws the fold as one region. Under the microscope it divides into four fields, OP1 to OP4. In two of them, touch to different parts of the body has been found to produce different responses. The atlas outline shows none of that.

  • Naidich et al. (2004): the insula and the tissue covering it, in dissection (a brain cut open by hand) and MRI.
  • Eickhoff et al. (2006): the four fields of the human parietal operculum.
  • Eickhoff et al. (2007): responses to touch in two of those fields.

The grid around the parietal operculum

Show cuts through the parietal operculum steps through every layer of the grid that contains the fold. A layer is one flat sheet of the grid, one voxel thick. There are 192 such layers: 66 sagittal, 73 coronal and 53 axial.

  • PaO and neighbours shows the fold with 6 mm of its surroundings. The whole cut shows the whole layer.
  • When the cuts first open, the coronal cut is at A = −18 mm, the sagittal cut at R = −44 mm and the axial cut at S = +12 mm.
  • A violet frame marks the layer in the 3D model, and tissue can stand in front of part of it.
  • If you mark a voxel and change the direction of the cut, the view of the cuts follows the voxel. When the new direction has no layer at that voxel, the view says where the voxel is and hides its mark. The index of layers.

Photographs of the same fold in dissected brains: Cullinane, Lim and Barry (2026), Fig. 1a–b and Mattos et al. (2008), Fig. 2A–B. They show which tissue covers which. They are not a measurement of how well the atlas fits a person.

Limits of the surfaces

  • One atlas, nobody’s brain. The atlas was drawn on an averaged reference brain, the ICBM 2009b nonlinear symmetric MNI152 average. Every border in the viewer is that atlas’s border. Where a border falls in a living person, and what any region does, need other evidence.
  • How the surfaces were made does not measure how accurate they are. Surfaces follow the 0.5 mm grid. The model is cut at the midline, and the cut face was closed with a constructed cap: points of a surface that lay beyond the midline were moved onto it, by at most 0.25 mm. The cap is this project’s work and is not part of the atlas. Most surfaces were then smoothed for display, by at most 0.125 mm, with the cap kept on the midline. The surfaces of the twenty regions drawn only when you ask were made the same way, on 21 November 2026. Those of VPL, VPM, the red nucleus and the parietal operculum were not smoothed.
  • The direction each surface faces, which is the side the viewer treats as its outside, was made consistent over each whole surface after the coordinates were converted. The record of that check, with the cases that go against it, is not part of this download.
  • The shapes of the basal forebrain, the septal nuclei and the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis, which the viewer’s list of regions offers as one ready-made group, have not been compared with any outside picture.
  • Merged surfaces hide their parts. 82 regions are drawn only as parts of larger surfaces and cannot be shown alone, some white matter (bundles of nerve fibres that join the regions) among them, such as the white matter of the hindbrain. Twenty regions are drawn only when you ask, among them the white matter of the forebrain, five of its fibre bundles and the nine fluid spaces (cavities that hold the fluid bathing the brain).
  • The atlas labels two voxels as hypothalamus (a small region at the base of the brain) far from the rest of it. They are in the model and are not drawn unless you choose Show the two voxels under Two stray voxels in the panel.
  • Another 3D program may show the colours lighter. The surface files store them as numbers that the viewer shows with its own palette (its set of colours).
