Backslash of the Brain · Simple guide

Backslash of the Brain is an Interoceptomics atlas that you can open on your computer. It shows places in the brain, spinal cord and organs. You can turn a model, look at images of tissue and follow a series of stops that explains how signals from the body can lead to a response.

Open the offline atlas

  1. Download Backslash-Atlas.zip. A ZIP holds many files in one download. Extract the whole ZIP and open the new folder called Backslash-Atlas. Keep the files and folders together so that the viewer can find its images and models.
  2. Start the viewer with the file for your computer. On Windows, open Windows.cmd. On macOS, open macOS.command. On Linux, open a terminal in the extracted folder, type sh Linux.sh and press Enter. The viewer needs Python 3.10 or newer and a current browser. If you want to start Python directly, type python3 launch.py or python launch.py in the extracted folder. Use the command that matches your Python installation.
  3. Keep the launcher window open. It opens a browser tab and prints the tab’s address. If you need to open it yourself, copy the whole printed address and paste it into your browser. That address points to your own computer. It can change the next time you start the atlas, so use the newly printed address each time. When you finish, press Ctrl+C in the launcher window to stop it.

Open the viewer through the launcher. The model and the PDF atlas are in the downloaded files and work without an internet connection. You need internet to follow links to outside source pages. The launch was verified in Chromium on Linux. The Windows and macOS start files were checked as code.

Explore the three anatomy views

Brain

Choose Brain and look for the Brain landmark menu. The starting choice, Medial left hemisphere, shows the inner face of the left half of the brain. Medial means toward the middle. At the starting angle, the front of the head is on the right of the picture.

Choose Sensory cortex landmark to find a fold that helps locate the area involved in touch and sensing body position. Choose Thalamic body relay · VPL to see a group of nerve cells deeper in the brain. A relay passes signals onward. This one passes body signals toward the outer brain. Its full name is the ventral posterolateral nucleus of the thalamus. Choose Insula under the opercula to see a part of the outer brain that is usually hidden by other folds.

Drag to turn the brain and scroll to zoom when 3D graphics are available. Choose 12° diagonal view to tilt the display and Reset view to return to the starting view. If the browser cannot draw the 3D brain, you can use the reference picture.

For some landmarks you can choose 3D surface or Coronal section. Think of a coronal section as a flat cut that faces the front of the head. The atlas divides the brain into small cubes called voxels. Each pixel on a cut covers half a millimetre along each displayed direction, and orange marks cubes with the selected label. More posterior moves toward the back of the head and More anterior moves toward the front. There are three available positions. Choose Inspect a highlighted voxel from this slice in all three planes to see a marked cube from that cut in three directions in the full atlas. From the surface view, Inspect this atlas voxel in all three planes, when shown, opens the landmark’s reference cube.

Brainstem sections

Choose Brainstem sections to see cross-sections of brainstem tissue. The brainstem joins the brain to the spinal cord. These pictures show tissue cut across the right half of one human donor’s brainstem, including the middle. Choose Section level to move to a named place such as Upper pons · parabrachial region. The pons is a part of the brainstem, and the label locates a group of cells in its upper part. Move between the five pictures with Previous section and Next section. Full-size plate ↗ opens the selected published picture on its own.

Cord & organs

Choose Cord & organs. Organ selection lets you see the spinal cord, heart, colon and urinary bladder together or look at one of them. The colon is part of the large bowel. The urinary bladder holds urine. Drag the 3D picture, or choose Rotate left or Rotate right. Reset view returns to its starting position. If you want still pictures or your browser does not draw 3D, choose Open the labelled organ views without 3D graphics. It takes you to front and back pictures of the selected structure. The shapes come from the female Human Reference Atlas assembly, a set of organs in their reference positions.

The separate Tissue plates button shows supporting images. Choose an item under Tissue reference and use Full-size plate ↗ to open it by itself. Read the caption to tell a picture taken through a microscope from a teaching diagram, and to see which species supplied the tissue.

Follow a circuit

  1. Open Follow a circuit and choose Muscle sensation to cortex. A circuit is a set of linked stops that can carry or pass on a signal. The first stop, Muscle spindle and tendon organ, shows two kinds of endings that respond to changes in a muscle or its tendon.
  2. Choose Next to reach Dorsal root and spinal cord. A dorsal root is one way sensory fibres enter the spinal cord. The later stops show the medulla, which is part of the brainstem, the VPL relay, and sensory cortex near the outside of the brain.
  3. Use Previous and Next, or select the stop you want by name. The picture changes to match the stop. To explore the pictures freely again, select a view button or choose a landmark from its menu.

Myoception is the programme’s name for sensing muscle-driven movement and the feedback that comes with it. For example, as you bend a knee you get signals about where your leg is. Skin touch can also provide feedback about the movement. Visceroception means sensing conditions inside an organ. The wall of the bladder stretches as it fills. That stretch can send information to the spinal cord.

The atlas lists routes in both directions. Organ sensation to forebrain follows information from an organ inward to parts of the brain. Cortex to skeletal muscle and Central commands to organs follow commands outward. The first goes toward muscles that move the skeleton. The second goes toward organs that carry out functions such as regulating blood pressure. Stretch reflex and coordination shows a response that can happen in the spinal cord and a separate branch toward the cerebellum, a region that helps coordinate movement.

Integration describes how incoming feedback and outgoing control work together. Choose Exercise and arterial pressure to follow signals from working muscles and see where the brainstem can influence the heart and blood vessels. When the pressure changes, sensors in some arteries respond and send information back. The loop links a bodily change with a new signal and a further response. You can also follow Bladder filling and emptying, A local gut reflex or Body state and selecting a response.

Read the evidence

The shapes and pictures come from three separate sources. The Allen Human Reference Atlas supplies the left brain and its labelled grid. Coulombe and colleagues supply close pictures of brainstem tissue from a different human donor. The female Human Reference Atlas assembly supplies the cord and organ shapes in their own reference positions. Changing the view changes which source you are seeing.

Use atlas colours to locate the named areas and read about roles described in published studies. Tissue pictures show what the tissue looks like. Steps in a circuit explain links from the studies. To find out which nerves actually fired, or to locate the exact active edge of a region in a person, use separate measurements. The atlas’s measurements describe anatomical structures, with each source in its own reference positions.
