Where the anatomy comes from, and how to read it

Every surface, every cut and every number in the viewer comes from one file: the grid of region numbers in the Allen Institute’s human reference atlas. This page says whose work that is, what this project did to it, and how the viewer’s positions and measurements are defined.

From that grid this project kept the labelled left half, drew surfaces around the regions, merged some regions and gave others a surface of their own, converted the coordinates, repacked the whole grid without loss for the cuts and the ruler, and added the colours, the cameras and the controls. The complete list of changes, and what you must keep when you reuse any file, is in THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES.html, in the folder you extracted the download into.

The 3D drawing is done by Three.js, © 2010–2026 Three.js authors, under the MIT licence.

Positions are in millimetres, on three axes

The atlas is a block of 394 × 466 × 378 cubes, each half a millimetre on a side. Each cube (a voxel) carries the number of one region, or 0 for none. A position names the centre of a voxel with three numbers:

AxisGrows towardsFirst voxel centre
Rthe right of the head. Negative R is the left side, R = 0 is the midline.−98 mm
Athe front of the head (anterior)−134 mm
Sthe top of the head (superior)−72 mm

Every position you type must sit on the grid: a multiple of 0.5 mm on each axis. The 3D model draws each point at (−R, S, A) ÷ 1000, in metres, so a mark in the model and a mark in a cut are the same place.

The three flat cuts read the whole grid

Explore full atlas labels shows three cuts through one voxel, straight from the grid, with nothing smoothed or filled in between voxels. The colours only tell neighbouring regions apart, and a colour can repeat; the name and number under the crosshair are the atlas’s own. A dark tile has no region. These cuts show region numbers, not tissue: the MRI picture the atlas was drawn on is not in the download.

The grid labels almost only the left side of the brain. Of its 6,999,201 labelled voxels, 6,974,625 lie left of the midline, 14,152 on it and 10,424 right of it. The cuts show all of them as recorded, with nothing mirrored. The 3D model draws the left side up to the midline; a teal cross marks your voxel there when the voxel has a region and lies on that side.

Find a structure knows the 141 regions that have voxels, on each side where they have them: 170 places in all. It lands on the voxel of the region that is nearest to the average position of all the region’s voxels on that side. A region that curves round its own middle has its average outside itself, and the finder still lands inside the region. If two voxels are equally near, it takes the one that comes first in the grid’s own order. The landing point is a place to start from, nothing more.

Volume of the selected label counts the voxels that carry exactly that number and multiplies by 0.125 mm³, the volume of one voxel. A voxel counts as left, midline or right by where its centre lies: R below 0, at 0, or above 0. A region and its sub-regions have separate numbers and are counted separately. A border one voxel wide is already half a millimetre across, so treat the volume as the atlas’s, at the atlas’s resolution.

The ruler is a straight line through the grid

Set A and Set B mark two voxel centres. The distance is the straight line between them, shown to three decimals although the grid itself steps in half millimetres. B minus A is the same step split along the three axes.

Follow the ruler through sections cuts that line wherever it crosses from one voxel into the next, reads the region number of each voxel it passes through from the whole grid, and joins neighbouring pieces that lie in the same region. A voxel the line only grazes at an edge or a corner adds no length. A and B sit at voxel centres, so the first and last voxels contribute only the part of the line inside them. If the line leaves a region and comes back, the region gets two rows.

The line measures a distance in the atlas’s reference brain. It is not the route of a nerve fibre.

VPL and VPM, voxel by voxel

The ventral posterior lateral nucleus (VPL, region 10424) and the ventral posterior medial nucleus (VPM, region 10425) lie side by side in the thalamus, all of both on the left of the grid.

VPM follows the inner edge of VPL: the centre of its voxels lies 5.39 mm nearer the midline than the centre of VPL’s. The two touch across 481 voxel faces, 120.25 mm² in all. Of those faces, 218 face left and right (54.5 mm²), 111 face front and back (27.75 mm²) and 152 face up and down (38 mm²). Voxels that meet only at an edge or a corner are not counted. The list of faces.

Show atlas sections reads a crop of the grid, 34 × 27 × 30 voxels, that keeps these two regions and blanks everything else. Blue is VPL, copper with white dots is VPM, a dark line follows the edges they share in that layer, and grey is anything else, which may well belong to another region. The sections open at A −20.5 mm (coronal), R −12.5 mm (sagittal) and S −2 mm (axial). The crop.

The atlas names two divisions of VPL (rostral and caudal) and one of VPM (parvocellular) and gives none of them voxels of its own, so the viewer cannot show them.

Published pictures of the same nuclei, for comparison by eye: Heckers et al., Figure 1 and Oishi et al., Figures 1–2: stained sections of the adult human thalamus, and MRI of brains after death. They are other brains, cut in other planes. No agreement with them has been measured.

The parietal operculum

The parietal operculum (PaO, region 13230) is the fold along the upper bank of the lateral sulcus, above the insula: it covers the insula from above. In the grid it has 43,319 voxels, 5,414.875 mm³, all on the left. Its surface in the model follows those voxels without smoothing. The surface file.

The atlas draws the fold as one region. Under the microscope it divides into four fields, OP1 to OP4, and touch to different parts of the body produces different responses across them. The atlas outline shows none of that.

  • Naidich et al. (2004): the insula and the tissue covering it, in dissection and MRI.
  • Eickhoff et al. (2006): the four fields of the human parietal operculum.
  • Eickhoff et al. (2007): responses to touch in two of those fields.

The grid around the parietal operculum

Show parietal operculum sections steps through every layer of the grid that contains the fold: 192 layers, 66 sagittal, 73 coronal and 53 axial. PaO and neighbours shows the fold with 6 mm of its surroundings. Full atlas plane shows the whole layer. The sections open at A −18 mm (coronal), R −44 mm (sagittal) and S +12 mm (axial). A violet frame marks the layer in the 3D model, and tissue can stand in front of part of it.

If you mark a voxel and change plane, the reader follows the voxel. When the new plane has no layer at that voxel, the reader says where the voxel is and hides its mark. The index of layers.

Photographs of the same fold in dissected brains: Cullinane, Lim and Barry (2026), Fig. 1a–b and Mattos et al. (2008), Fig. 2A–B. They show which tissue covers which. They are not a measurement of how well the atlas fits a person.

What the surfaces can and cannot tell you

  • One atlas, nobody’s brain. The atlas was drawn on an averaged reference brain, the ICBM 2009b nonlinear symmetric MNI152 average. Every border in the viewer is that atlas’s border. Where a border falls in a living person, and what any region does, need other evidence.
  • How the surfaces were made is not how accurate they are. Surfaces follow the half-millimetre grid. The cut face at the midline was closed with a constructed cap of at most 0.25 mm, and an earlier display step moved surfaces by at most 0.125 mm. The direction each surface faces was corrected as a whole; local disagreements between the grid and the smoothed surface remain. The shapes of the basal forebrain, the septal nuclei and the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis have not been compared with any outside picture.
  • Merged surfaces hide their parts. 82 regions are drawn only as parts of larger surfaces and cannot be lifted out, and 20 regions have no surface at all.
  • The atlas labels two voxels as hypothalamus far from the rest of it. They are in the model and not drawn unless you choose Show the two voxels under Two stray voxels in the panel.
  • Another 3D program may show the colours lighter. The surface files store them as numbers that the viewer shows with its own palette.

Published pictures the whole model was compared with, by eye: Ding et al. (2016), the cell-level atlas that supplied the tissue sections and the names (erratum); Wang et al. (2011) and Güdük et al. (2020), dissections of the insula and the deep structures; Emmi et al. (2020), stained tissue of the subthalamic region. None of their pictures is in the download.
