Where the anatomy comes from, and how to read it · Simple edition

The Interoceptomics Atlas Viewer draws a 3D model of the left half of the human brain. It makes the model from one published brain atlas. A brain atlas is a map that divides the brain into named regions.

Every surface, every cut and every number in the viewer comes from one file of that atlas: the grid of region numbers in the Allen Institute’s human reference atlas. This page says three things:

  • whose work the file is;
  • what this project did to it;
  • how the viewer’s positions and measurements are defined.

Four words are used on this page. A region is one named part of the atlas, and each region has a number. A voxel is one of the small cubes that the atlas is built from. A cut is a flat slice through those cubes. A surface is the skin that the viewer draws around a region, or around several regions merged together.

The atlas is the Allen Institute’s work

The atlas is the Allen Human Reference Atlas — 3D, 2020, version 1.0.0. Its authors are Song-Lin Ding, Joshua J. Royall, Susan M. Sunkin, Benjamin A. C. Facer, Phil Lesnar, Amy Bernard, Lydia Ng and Ed S. Lein, and its year is 2020. Its number in a public register of research resources is RRID:SCR_017764. Its copyright line is: Copyright © 2019 Allen Institute for Brain Science.

The atlas is licensed under CC BY 4.0. That licence lets anyone copy and adapt the work if they give the credit, link the licence, keep the notices and say what they changed. The atlas’s own README, the document of notes that comes with its files, states the licence as effective 1 September 2022.

The atlas was adapted by the Interoceptomics project for this viewer. The Allen Institute has not endorsed the viewer. The Allen Institute offers the original atlas files, and among them the grid itself, a file named annotation.nii.gz.

This project did six things with that grid:

  1. It kept the labelled left half.
  2. It drew surfaces around the regions.
  3. It merged some regions into larger surfaces and gave others a surface of their own.
  4. It converted the coordinates, which are the numbers that say where a point is.
  5. It repacked the whole grid for the cuts and the ruler, without loss: it stored the grid in another form and kept every region number.
  6. It added the colours, the cameras and the controls. A camera is a viewpoint from which the model is drawn.

The page Licences and credits has the complete list of changes. It also says what you must keep when you reuse any file.

The 3D drawing is done by Three.js, © 2010–2026 Three.js authors, under the MIT licence (a plain-text file). That licence lets anyone reuse the code if they keep its copyright and permission notice.

A position is three numbers, in millimetres

The atlas is a block of small cubes: 394 × 466 × 378 of them along its three directions. Each cube is 0.5 mm (half a millimetre) on a side. A cube is called a voxel. Each voxel carries the number of one region, or the number 0 if it belongs to no region.

A position names the centre of one voxel. It does so with three numbers, one for each direction. The directions are called axes, and their letters are R, A and S:

AxisThe number grows towardsCentre of the first voxel
Rthe right of the head. A negative R is on the left side. R = 0 is the midline, the flat boundary down the middle of the head, between its left and right halves.−98 mm
Athe front of the head. A stands for anterior, which means towards the front.−134 mm
Sthe top of the head. S stands for superior, which means towards the top.−72 mm

Every position you type must sit on the grid. Each of the three numbers must be a multiple of 0.5 mm, for example 12, 12.5 or 13.

The 3D model keeps the same places in another form. It works in metres, and it puts the three numbers in another order: it draws each point at (−R, S, A) ÷ 1000. That is R with its sign reversed, then S, then A, each divided by 1000 to turn millimetres into metres. The conversion is always the same, so a mark in the model and a mark in a cut are the same place.

The 3D model is different, because you can turn it freely. There, look at the three letters at the corner of the picture. They show which way each axis points. When R points straight towards you, it reads R ⊙; when it points straight away from you, it reads R ⊗.

The three flat cuts read the whole grid

The button Open three flat cuts shows three cuts through one voxel. They come straight from the grid. Nothing is smoothed, and nothing is filled in between voxels.

  • The colours in these cuts only tell neighbouring regions apart. A colour can repeat, so two regions far apart may share one. They are not the colour groups of the 3D model.
  • The name and the number shown under the crosshair are the atlas’s own. The crosshair is the mark where the three cuts cross.
  • Each small square in a cut is one voxel, and is called a tile. A dark tile is a voxel that has no region.
  • The cuts show region numbers. They do not show tissue. The atlas was drawn on an MRI picture, which is a scanner’s picture of the inside of the head, and that picture is not part of this viewer.

The grid labels almost only the left side of the brain. It has 6,999,201 labelled voxels, which are the voxels that belong to a region. Of these, 6,974,625 lie left of the midline, 14,152 lie on it, and 10,424 lie right of it. The cuts show all of them as the atlas file holds them. Nothing is mirrored from one side to the other.

The 3D model draws the left side up to the midline. When your voxel has a region and lies on that side, a teal cross marks it in the 3D model.

How the finder chooses where to land

Find a region, in the three flat cuts, knows the 141 regions that have voxels. It knows each of them on every side where it has voxels, which makes 170 places in all.

  1. For a region and a side, the finder works out the average position of all the region’s voxels on that side.
  2. It lands on the voxel of the region that is nearest to that average.
  3. If two voxels are equally near, it takes the one that comes first in the grid’s own order.

Some regions curve round their own middle, so that the average falls outside the region. The finder still lands inside the region, because it always lands on one of the region’s own voxels. The landing point is a place to start from, and nothing more.

How a volume is counted

Volume of the selected region counts the voxels that carry exactly that region’s number. It multiplies the count by 0.125 mm³, the volume of one voxel. The unit mm³ is the cubic millimetre.

  • Each voxel counts as left, midline or right. The centre of the voxel decides: R below 0 is left, R at 0 is midline, and R above 0 is right.
  • A region and its sub-regions have separate numbers. They are counted separately.
  • A border one voxel wide is already 0.5 mm across. Borders follow the grid, so the volume is approximate.

The ruler is a straight line through the grid

Set A and Set B mark two points. Each is the centre of a voxel. The distance is the length of the straight line between them. It is shown to three decimals, although the grid itself steps in half millimetres. B minus A is the same step, split along the three axes.

Follow the ruler through the cuts says which regions the line passes through. It works in three steps:

  1. It splits the line wherever the line crosses from one voxel into the next.
  2. For each voxel that the line passes through, it reads the region number from the whole grid.
  3. It joins neighbouring pieces that lie in the same region into one stretch.

Three things follow from that:

  • A voxel that the line only grazes, at an edge or a corner, adds no length.
  • A and B sit at voxel centres, so the first and the last voxel contribute only the part of the line that is inside them.
  • If the line leaves a region and later comes back to it, the region gets two rows.

The line measures a distance in the atlas’s reference brain. It is not the route of a nerve fibre.

VPL and VPM, voxel by voxel

VPL is short for the ventral posterior lateral nucleus. It is region 10424. VPM is short for the ventral posterior medial nucleus. It is region 10425. A nucleus is a cluster of nerve cells. These two lie side by side in the thalamus, deep in the middle of the brain. Every voxel of both is on the left of the grid.

The two regions touch. A voxel is a cube, and a face is one of its six flat sides. Where a voxel of VPL and a voxel of VPM lie face to face, they share that face. There are 481 shared faces, with an area of 120.25 mm² in all. The unit mm² is the square millimetre. Voxels that meet only at an edge or a corner are not counted.

Show cuts through VPL and VPM reads a crop of the grid. A crop is a cut-out block. This one is 34 × 27 × 30 voxels. It keeps these two regions and blanks everything else. In its cuts:

  • blue is VPL;
  • copper with white dots is VPM;
  • a dark line follows the edges that the two share in that layer;
  • grey is anything else. A grey voxel may belong to another region.

When the cuts first open, the coronal cut is at A = −20.5 mm, the sagittal cut at R = −12.5 mm and the axial cut at S = −2 mm. The viewer keeps the crop as a file.

The atlas names two divisions of VPL, called rostral and caudal: a front part and a back part. It names one division of VPM, called parvocellular: a part made of small cells. The atlas gives none of these divisions voxels of its own. The viewer therefore cannot show them.

Two published sets of pictures show the same nuclei, for comparison by eye: Heckers et al., Figure 1 and Oishi et al., Figures 1–2. They are stained sections of the adult human thalamus, and MRI of brains after death. A stained section is a thin slice of tissue coloured so that its structure shows under a microscope. These are other brains, cut in other planes. No agreement between them and this atlas has been measured.

The parietal operculum

The parietal operculum is region 13230, and its abbreviation is PaO. The lateral sulcus is the deep groove along the side of the brain. The parietal operculum is the back part of the upper bank of that groove, which is the wall of brain above it. The fold lies above the back part of the insula and covers it from above. The insula is a stretch of the brain’s outer layer that lies folded deep inside the side of the brain. Further forward, other regions of the atlas cover the insula from above, chiefly the frontal operculum and the opercular part of the inferior frontal gyrus. In the viewer they are parts of a larger surface.

In the grid the parietal operculum has 43,319 voxels. That is 5,414.875 mm³. All of them are on the left. Its surface in the model follows those voxels without smoothing. The viewer keeps the surface as a file.

The atlas draws the fold as one region. Under the microscope the fold divides into four fields, named OP1 to OP4. In two of those fields, touch to different parts of the body has been found to produce different responses. The atlas outline shows none of that.

  • Naidich et al. (2004): the insula and the tissue covering it, in dissection and MRI. A dissection is a brain cut open by hand so that its parts can be seen.
  • Eickhoff et al. (2006): the four fields of the human parietal operculum.
  • Eickhoff et al. (2007): responses to touch in two of those fields.

The grid around the parietal operculum

Show cuts through the parietal operculum steps through every layer of the grid that contains the fold. A layer is one flat sheet of the grid, one voxel thick. There are 192 such layers: 66 sagittal, 73 coronal and 53 axial.

  • You can choose how much of each layer to see. PaO and neighbours shows the fold with 6 mm of its surroundings. The whole cut shows the whole layer.
  • When the cuts first open, the coronal cut is at A = −18 mm, the sagittal cut at R = −44 mm and the axial cut at S = +12 mm.
  • A violet frame marks the layer in the 3D model. Tissue can stand in front of part of the frame.
  • If you mark a voxel and then change the direction of the cut, the view of the cuts follows the voxel. Sometimes the new direction has no layer at that voxel. The view then says where the voxel is and hides its mark. The viewer keeps the index of layers as a file.

Two papers have photographs of the same fold in dissected brains: Cullinane, Lim and Barry (2026), Fig. 1a–b and Mattos et al. (2008), Fig. 2A–B. They show which tissue covers which. They are not a measurement of how well the atlas fits a person.

What the surfaces can tell you, and what they cannot

  • It is one atlas, and nobody’s brain. The atlas was drawn on an averaged reference brain: one brain image made by averaging brain scans. Its name is the ICBM 2009b nonlinear symmetric MNI152 average. Every border in the viewer is that atlas’s border. Where a border falls in a living person, and what any region does, need other evidence.
  • How the surfaces were made does not say how accurate they are. The surfaces follow the 0.5 mm grid. The model is cut at the midline, and the cut face was closed with a constructed cap: points of a surface that lay beyond the midline were moved onto it, by at most 0.25 mm. The cap is this project’s work. It is not part of the atlas. After that, most surfaces were smoothed for display: the smoothing moved no point more than 0.125 mm and kept the cap on the midline. The surfaces of the twenty regions drawn only when you ask were made the same way, on 21 November 2026. The surfaces of VPL, VPM, the red nucleus and the parietal operculum were not smoothed.
  • Each surface has an inner and an outer side, and the viewer shades it by which side faces out. Which side faces out was made the same over the whole of each surface, after the coordinates were converted. The record of how that was checked, with the cases that go against it, is not part of this download.
  • Three shapes have not been compared with any outside picture: the basal forebrain, the septal nuclei and the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis. The viewer’s list of regions offers the three as one ready-made group.
  • Merged surfaces hide their parts. 82 regions are drawn only as parts of larger surfaces and cannot be shown alone. Some white matter is among them, such as the white matter of the hindbrain. White matter is the bundles of nerve fibres that join the regions to one another. Twenty regions are drawn only when you ask. Among them are the white matter of the forebrain, five of its fibre bundles and the nine fluid spaces, which are the cavities that hold the fluid bathing the brain.
  • The atlas labels two voxels as hypothalamus far from the rest of the hypothalamus, which is a small region at the base of the brain. The two voxels are in the model. They are not drawn unless you choose Show the two voxels, which is under Two stray voxels in the panel. The panel is the column of controls beside the model.
  • Another 3D program may show the colours lighter. The surface files store the colours as numbers, and the viewer shows those numbers with its own palette, which is its set of colours.

Apart from the basal forebrain, the septal nuclei and the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis, the model was compared by eye with published pictures. They are: Ding et al. (2016), the cell-level atlas that supplied the tissue sections and the names (with an erratum, which is a published correction); Wang et al. (2011) and Güdük et al. (2020), dissections of the insula and the deep structures; and Emmi et al. (2020), stained tissue of the subthalamic region, the part of the brain just below the thalamus. None of their pictures is included here.
