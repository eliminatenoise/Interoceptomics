Interoceptomics Atlas Viewer · Technical guide

The Interoceptomics Atlas Viewer shows, in a web browser, a 3D model of the left half of the human brain. The model can be turned, cut through and measured, and 54 of its regions can be shown on their own. It is made from a brain atlas: a published map that divides the brain into named regions. The atlas is the Allen Institute’s reference atlas of the human brain. The model has 141 named regions on a grid of 0.5 mm cubes. Three colours mark the regions that this project links to movement and touch (blue), to the automatic running of the organs (copper), and to where the two meet (purple). The project is Interoceptomics, a proposed field of science that joins the body’s sense of its own interior (for example of a heartbeat or a breath) to the mapping of nerve connections.

The viewer is a picture of published anatomy, and its colours are a reading of published sources. No brain activity was recorded for it, and nothing in it has been validated, which means confirmed by tests. The limits are listed under Limits of the viewer.

Open the viewer on the website or from the download

The same viewer, file for file, is on the project’s website and in a download. The download is a ZIP file, for use without an internet connection.

The model is a 66 MB file. On the website the first view downloads about 38 MB, because the website sends the files compressed. The wait for it was timed on 25 November 2026 in Chromium on a desktop computer, with the link held to a set speed: about 40 seconds at 9 Mbit/s, and about three and a quarter minutes at 1.6 Mbit/s, the speed of a slow phone network. It was not timed on a phone or on the published website.

Until the model is ready, the space where it will appear shows a pale still picture of the brain, which cannot be turned, and counts the megabytes as they arrive, and the panel says that the 3D controls are still locked. The panel is the column of controls beside the model, or below it on a phone. The three flat cuts work before the model is ready.

First view

Under Starting view, choose Insula exposed. The outer surface of the brain is hidden and the insula is shown in purple. The insula is a stretch of the brain’s outer layer (the cortex) that lies folded deep inside the side of the brain, hidden under the folds that cover it. Drag to turn the model. Three more things to try are listed below.

Controls

The project assigned each colour from what published studies report. In that reading, the parts of the brain that move the body and the parts that run its organs are not separate: they overlap. The viewer marks the overlap on the atlas and lets you take the atlas apart to look at it. The marks are a reading of published sources, not a recording.

A name in bold is a name on the screen. Six terms are used throughout:

  • A region is one named part of the atlas. Its atlas number is the number that the atlas gives it.
  • A voxel is one 0.5 mm cube of the atlas grid. Every voxel carries the number of one region, or 0 for none.
  • A cut is a flat slice through that grid. A tile is one voxel as a cut shows it.
  • A surface is the skin that the viewer draws around a region, or around several regions merged together.

Three things to try

  1. Type PaO into Find a region, pick the parietal operculum from the list under it and choose Show it alone. The parietal operculum is the fold that covers the back part of the insula from above; it is shown on its own. Picked in Insula exposed, it turns the view to Outer face (lateral). Show the whole brain brings the rest back.
  2. Type VPL, pick it, and choose Compare VPL and VPM, then Show the faces they share. The view shows two neighbouring regions deep in the brain, with gold marking where the atlas has them touch. Return to prior view puts everything back.
  3. Choose Open three flat cuts. The view shows three cuts through the atlas grid itself. Pick a tile in one cut and the other two cuts follow.

The brain has a left and a right half. The atlas labels almost only the left one, so every view shows the left half. Starting view offers four views:

ViewWhat it shows
Inner face (medial)The flat inner face, where the two halves of the brain meet. Medial means towards the midline, the boundary between the left and right halves of the head.
Outer face (lateral)The outside of the left half. Lateral means towards the side.
Insula exposedThe whole outer surface is hidden to show the insula. That hides far more than the folds that cover the insula in a real brain.
Deep regionsThe outer surface and the insula are both hidden. What is left stays where it sits in the head, so one deep region can still stand in front of another.

A small picture of the inner face and three letters stay on screen for orientation: A points to the front of the head, S up, R to the right. R pointing straight towards you reads R ⊙, and straight away from you R ⊗; the small picture’s caption uses the same marks.

The four views leave out most of the white matter and all the fluid spaces. White matter is the bundles of nerve fibres that join the regions to one another. The views leave out the white matter of the forebrain and five of its fibre bundles; some other white matter is drawn, as part of larger surfaces. The forebrain is the largest part of the brain: the two cerebral hemispheres (the large folded masses that make up most of each half of the brain) and the parts deep between them. The fluid spaces are the cavities that hold the fluid bathing the brain: the ventricles and the canals that join them, which the atlas divides into nine regions. These fifteen regions (the white matter of the forebrain, which the atlas counts as one region, with its five fibre bundles and the nine fluid spaces) hold almost a third of the atlas’s labelled voxels (2,238,813 of 6,999,201), which are the voxels that belong to a region.

Colour groups

Colour groups chooses the colours:

ChoiceColours
All three groupsBlue, copper and purple at once.
Somatic: movement and touchBlue: regions linked to moving the body and feeling touch.
Autonomic: running the organsCopper: regions linked to the automatic running of the organs.
Integration: where the two meetPurple: regions that this project places where the two meet. Every coloured region is one of them.
No coloursPlain anatomy.

A region is drawn in one colour at a time. With All three groups, a region that belongs to more than one group has the colour of one of its groups, so the colour alone does not give all its groups. With a single group chosen, every region that belongs to it takes that group’s colour.

Which region gets which colour is this project’s reading of published sources, not a measurement. The viewer lists 54 sources, and 49 of them are cited for at least one region. To see the reading for one region, select it and open Evidence and limits: why the region is coloured or left plain, what its outline leaves out, and links to the sources. The line under the region’s name lists all its colour groups, or says that it has none.

Those three groups are all 141 of the atlas’s regions. Picking from the list can change Starting view: a region of the insula, or the whole insula, turns it to Insula exposed; anything else under Can be shown alone turns it to Deep regions, except the regions whose Zoom to it waits for Show it alone, which keep the view; a cortical region that is part of a larger surface, the parietal operculum among them, picked in Insula exposed or Deep regions turns it to Outer face (lateral), which shows little or nothing of many of them: those on the inner face or the underside (for example the cingulate gyrus and the precuneus) and those deep in the groove along the side (for example the frontal operculum). Use Inner face (medial) for those on the inner face; the flat cuts show every region. Show the whole brain then returns to the view that is chosen.

Twenty of the regions are drawn only when you ask: the white matter of the forebrain and five of its fibre bundles, the nine fluid spaces, the four regions of the smell pathway (which carries smell from the nose into the brain), and the claustrum (a thin sheet of grey matter). Pick one and choose Show it alone; its surface loads when you pick it. Every region can also be read in the flat cuts.

Three names in the list end in “(parts not named separately)”: the putamen, the basal forebrain and the amygdaloid complex. Each is what the atlas leaves under the region’s own number after it has named the parts inside it.

Compare VPL and VPM

The ventral posterior lateral nucleus (VPL) and the ventral posterior medial nucleus (VPM) are two sensory nuclei that lie side by side in the thalamus, deep in the middle of the brain. In their names ventral means lower, posterior further back, lateral nearer the side and medial nearer the midline. Select either, then choose Compare VPL and VPM. The two surfaces appear together, VPL in blue and VPM in copper.

Open three flat cuts shows three cuts that cross at one voxel: one parallel to the face (coronal), one parallel to the side of the head (sagittal) and one horizontal (axial). The cuts read the whole atlas grid, so they show all 141 regions, including the little that the atlas labels on the right side.

Measure between two voxels

The ruler, which the screen heads Measure between two voxels, is in two places: under Show cuts through the parietal operculum and, in the VPL and VPM comparison, under Show cuts through VPL and VPM. It is not in the three flat cuts.

  1. Mark a voxel: pick a tile, or type its position under Go to a position and choose Go to voxel. Choose Set A.
  2. Mark another voxel and choose Set B. The panel gives B minus A along each axis and the Straight-line distance, in millimetres. Both points stay while you change cuts.
  3. Follow the ruler through the cuts lists how much of the line lies in each region, in order from A to B. Choose a row to jump to where that stretch begins.
  4. Clear A and B starts again.

Under Follow the ruler through the cuts, the slider Voxel along the line, A to B, with Previous voxel and Next voxel, steps through the voxels the line passes through. The cut opens at each, and an amber cross marks its centre; the line can pass to one side of that centre. After the cut is moved away, Show this voxel returns to the line.

The line is a straight line through the atlas’s reference brain. It is not the path of a nerve fibre and not a guide for anything clinical (the care of patients).

Limits of the viewer

The viewer shows where a published atlas draws each region. It does not show what any region does. The panel’s line What the model leaves out lists several of these limits.

  • One reference brain, left half. The atlas was drawn on an averaged reference brain (the ICBM 2009b nonlinear symmetric MNI152 average), not on a person. It is nobody’s brain. The atlas labels almost only the left side: all 141 regions have voxels on the left, 22 have some on the midline and 7 have some on the right. The 3D model draws the left side up to the midline.
  • The colours are a reading, not a recording. A region can belong to more than one group. No colour does not mean that a region does nothing, only that this project gave it no colour group: 81 of the atlas’s 141 regions carry none.
  • The grid steps in 0.5 mm. Borders follow the atlas’s cubes, so small regions look blocky. Most surfaces were smoothed a little, and the smoothing moved no point more than 0.125 mm; the surfaces of VPL, VPM, the red nucleus and the parietal operculum were not smoothed. Where the model is cut at the midline, points beyond the midline were moved onto it, by at most 0.25 mm.
  • The atlas gives no voxels of their own to the small nuclei that regulate the organs, so they are not drawn separately. They lie in the brainstem (the stalk at the base of the brain that joins the rest of the brain to the spinal cord) and in the hypothalamus (a small region at the base of the brain). The same holds for the separate deep nuclei of the cerebellum (the folded part at the back of the brain, below the hemispheres), which the atlas has only together, as one region, the cerebellar deep nuclei.
  • The atlas does not mark the working fields of the cortex, the areas defined by the work they do, such as the field that first receives touch from the body. A coloured stretch of cortex is the atlas’s outline, not a field that has been shown to do that work.
  • Nothing in the viewer has been validated, scientifically or clinically. Distances and volumes describe the atlas’s reference brain, not a patient. The viewer holds anatomy and code, and no recordings.

The atlas labels two voxels as hypothalamus far from the rest of it. They are drawn only after Show the two voxels, under the panel’s line Two stray voxels.
