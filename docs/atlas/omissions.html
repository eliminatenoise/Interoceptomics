The twenty regions drawn only when you ask · Simple edition

The Interoceptomics Atlas Viewer draws a 3D model of the left half of the human brain. It makes the model from a published brain atlas, which is a map that divides the brain into named regions.

The viewer draws twenty of the atlas’s regions only when you ask for them. They are not small: together they hold almost a third of the atlas’s labelled voxels, which are the small cubes that the atlas gives to a region. This page says which regions they are, why they stay off at first, how to see them, and how their surfaces were made.

Which regions they are

  • The white matter of the forebrain, and five of its named bundles of nerve fibres. White matter is the bundles of nerve fibres that join the regions to one another. The forebrain is the largest part of the brain: the two cerebral hemispheres, which are the large folded masses that make up most of each half of the brain, and the parts deep between them.
  • Nine fluid spaces: the ventricles and the canals that join them. These are cavities that hold fluid. They are not tissue.
  • The four regions of the smell pathway. This pathway carries smell from the nose into the brain.
  • The claustrum. It is a thin sheet of grey matter between the insula and the putamen. Grey matter is tissue where the bodies of the nerve cells lie. The insula is a stretch of the brain’s outer layer, folded deep inside the side of the brain. The putamen is a region of grey matter deep inside each half of the brain.

None of the twenty carries a colour. The viewer’s three colours mark the regions that this project links to movement and touch (blue), to the automatic running of the organs (copper), and to where the two meet (purple). These twenty are drawn in grey.

The 3D model has 64 surfaces. A surface is the skin that the viewer draws around a region, or around several regions merged together. The atlas has 141 regions, and every one of them is drawn, on a surface of its own or as part of a larger one. There is one exception: five small nuclei that lie inside the thalamus. A nucleus is a cluster of nerve cells, and the thalamus is a mass of grey matter deep in the middle of the brain.

Why they stay off until you ask

The atlas is built from small cubes called voxels. A voxel is one 0.5 mm cube of the grid, which is half a millimetre on a side. The atlas has 6,999,201 labelled voxels, which are the voxels that belong to a region.

  • The twenty regions together hold 2,255,724 of those voxels.
  • The white matter of the forebrain alone holds 2,089,673.

They stay off until you ask for two reasons: so that the first view stays as it was before these surfaces were added, and so that nothing more is downloaded until you ask.

  • The button described below draws fifteen of the twenty. Those fifteen are a 36 MB download. MB stands for megabyte, a measure of the size of a file.
  • The white matter of the forebrain alone is 32.2 MB.

When they are on, the white matter of the forebrain and the fluid spaces are drawn see-through. Drawn solid, they would hide what the viewer is for:

  • The white matter wraps the coloured regions.
  • The fluid spaces on the midline lie in front of the thalamus and the brainstem when you look at the brain from its inner face. The midline is the flat boundary down the middle of the head, between its left and right halves. The brainstem is the stalk at the base of the brain that joins the rest of the brain to the spinal cord.

Three ways to see them

  1. All in place. Choose Show white matter and fluid spaces. The button is under Reset view. It draws fifteen of the regions where they sit, in every view: the white matter of the forebrain, five of its fibre bundles and the nine fluid spaces. That is a 36 MB download in all. They show best with the view Deep regions.
  2. One on its own. This works for any one of the twenty. Type its name, its abbreviation or its atlas number into Find a region. An atlas number is the number that the atlas gives a region. Pick the region from the list and choose Show it alone. Its file loads when you pick it.
  3. Voxel by voxel. Choose Open three flat cuts, and then use Find a region there. A cut is a flat slice through the grid.

The table lists the twenty regions in four groups. For each region it gives the atlas number, the abbreviation, the name, the number of voxels, and the size of the download in megabytes. On a narrow screen, slide the table sideways to see all five columns.

How their surfaces were made

The surfaces were made from the same atlas grid as the rest of the model. The 54 regions that can be shown alone have their surfaces in three places. The main model file, which is the file of the 3D model that the viewer loads first, holds the surfaces of thirty of them. These twenty each have a file of their own, which loads when you ask. Four more have a file of their own each: VPL, VPM, the red nucleus and the parietal operculum. The twenty surfaces on this page were made the same way as the thirty in the main model file:

  1. A surface was drawn around the region’s voxels on the 0.5 mm grid.
  2. The surface was closed at the midline, where the model is cut. Points that lay beyond the midline were moved onto it, by at most 0.25 mm.
  3. The surface was smoothed slightly. In the smoothing no point moved more than 0.125 mm.

Four things are worth knowing about the result:

  • Some of these regions touch a region that already had a surface. There the atlas gives the two regions the same boundary. After smoothing, the two surfaces stay within a quarter of a millimetre of each other at that boundary.
  • The 3D model is the left half of the grid, up to the midline. Five of these regions also have voxels right of the midline. Those voxels are not drawn. They belong to: the white matter of the forebrain (7,832 voxels), the third ventricle (1,341), the fourth ventricle (953), the cerebral aqueduct (224) and the anterior commissure (15).
  • One surface sticks out. The central canal is one of the fluid spaces, and the medulla is the lowest part of the brainstem. At the bottom of the grid the atlas starts the central canal 1 mm lower than the medulla. For the lowest 2.5 mm of the canal, no other region lies within 1.5 mm of it. So the surface of the central canal sticks out below the brainstem there.
  • The viewer’s list of regions keeps a proposed colour group, Integration (the purple group, which the viewer calls “where the two meet”), for three of the twenty: the olfactory bulb, the anterior olfactory nucleus and the piriform cortex. It is a proposal only. Nothing is drawn for it, and the line under each of these names reads No colour group. Only Evidence and limits mentions the proposal.

Whose names and numbers these are

The four groups in the table are this project’s. The names, the abbreviations and the atlas numbers are the Allen Institute’s. They come from its table of regions, and a copy of that table travels with the viewer: sources/allen-version1/voxel_count.csv.

The voxel counts on this page were recounted from the atlas grid. They equal the counts in the Allen Institute’s table.
