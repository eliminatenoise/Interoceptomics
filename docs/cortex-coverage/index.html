What a dot tells you

These five recording layouts supplied a separate test of finger-movement prediction. Four people supplied training data, then the model was tested on the fifth after calibration. See how the forecasts changed across people.

A dot marks a recording contact. Its colour comes from the anatomical code beside that contact in the original file. To see how electrical power changed during a finger cue, open the single-person activity reader for D01.

How the two sides of a recording match

In each original MATLAB file, row n of locs gives the position of recording channel n. Row n of elec_regions gives its anatomical code. Both use the channel order left after the authors removed bad channels. The points share the original frame with brain.vert and brain.tri. We keep every source vertex, face and contact. Only face indexing changes, from one-based to zero-based.

The initial view uses the participant’s angles from call_subs_figs.m. Each brain is centred and fitted to its own display. The files give no coordinate units or scanner transform. Distances on the screen cannot establish an anatomical correspondence across people.

What the region names cover

“Dorsal motor” and “dorsal sensory” are the study’s labels for dorsal primary motor and primary somatosensory cortex. “Ventral sensorimotor” spans both. “Rolandic” refers to the area around the central sulcus, with no finer assignment. “Frontal” and “parietal” here exclude the Rolandic region. The study gives no anatomical name for codes 0 and 5. Each keeps its original number.

D01–D05 are the five preselected development recordings, in the release-code order bp, cc, ht, jc and jp. The counts describe these clinical implants. They cannot estimate how much of the whole cortex a region occupies or how strongly it responded. An implant’s broad location in the study notes can differ from the labels attached to its individual contacts.

Study, rights and ethics

Kai Joshua Miller deposited the FingerFlex dataset in the Stanford Digital Repository. Miller, Hermes, Honey, Hebb, Ramsey, Knight, Ojemann and Fetz reported the original experiment in 2012. The published comparison counts their anatomical codes. The software reads anatomy on request from the selected original file. The anatomy and derived counts carry CC BY-SA 4.0. Credit the authors, identify adaptations and preserve the licence. Respect participant privacy and confidentiality, including the prohibition on identifying individuals. The project’s original code carries CC0.
