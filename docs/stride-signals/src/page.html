What went into the average?

Schreiber and Moissenet asked people to walk barefoot over a walkway at different speeds. This view uses the 0.4–0.8 m/s condition. The original files label two right heel strikes that enclose a stride. We placed each participant’s walk on a common forward, left and up axis and aligned the strides by their fraction complete. A person who supplied three usable walks counts as much as a person who supplied one.

To see how far a wrist or ankle went in a fifth of a second, start at the nearest stored frame inside each stride and count forward exactly 20 frames. Both ends must fall inside that same stride. Near the next footfall, shorter strides drop out first. The direction and distance use only people who supplied a valid pair. Opposing movements can cancel in the arrow while still contributing to the distance readout.

Force belongs to the platform a foot actually hit. A plate can read zero while the person is standing somewhere else. The optional right-contact traces need supporting evidence from the source assignment, foot markers and contact timing. The instrument records an external force. No part of this recording measures someone’s sensation, an individual muscle’s force or a spinal signal.

Source, processing and reuse

The original files store optical positions at 100 Hz and electrical and platform channels at 1,500 Hz. The published signals were filtered using samples from both sides of each time point. The curves are retrospective averages. A gap means fewer than three walkers contributed. Select a gap to see how many had a measurement. The shaded band shows variation across the contributing people.

Céline Schreiber and Florent Moissenet (2019), A multimodal dataset of human gait at different walking speeds established on injury-free adult participants.
