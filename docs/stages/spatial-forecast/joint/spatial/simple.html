Forecast a movement in three dimensions

Choose a recording in the 3D viewer. Select a wrist, shoulder, elbow, hip, knee or ankle. The dark body shows the current positions. The green outline shows where the forecast puts them 120 milliseconds later, about one eighth of a second. Choose Reveal later measurement to show the orange crosses. The distance between a forecast and its cross is the error, in millimetres.

Use the viewer and the game

Use Turn left and Turn right to look at the same forecast from another angle. The recorded points and the forecast stay in place. Move the time slider to choose a different moment. Select Relative to hips to put the midpoint of the two hips at the centre of each pose. Someone walking across a room can move a long way in room coordinates while a wrist stays at nearly the same distance from the hips.

The three Carnegie Mellon examples show optical markers attached to a moving person. Two further examples come from an overground walking study. Those recordings include electrical signals measured on the right leg. Select one of the walks and tick Use the recorded right-leg muscle signals to see whether they change a forecast. The display starts with the signals hidden. A muscle recording measures electrical activity. Measuring the force that the muscle produces requires other information.

In NeuroSurge Full or Lite, press F, enter forecast in 3D, and press Enter. You can also press C, select Forecast a tracked point and expand 3D motion forecast. Choose a body point and select Start camera when you want to begin capture. You can use a recorded video or timestamped stills instead. Keep both hips and the point of interest visible. The grey and green outlines show positions inferred from a single camera. Turn this small drawing with its own buttons. Its controls keep the game camera in place.

The live panel starts with Keep the last position. Select Trained 3D model to compare it. The later camera reading checks how closely the forecast followed the tracker. That comparison still contains the tracker's own errors. In a separate test we could also use laboratory measurements to find out how far the tracker itself missed.

Use your own file

Extract the complete portable ZIP. With Node.js 18 or later installed, open the launcher for your operating system. Linux and Chromium were used for the execution checks. The package also contains Windows and macOS launchers. To see a result as numbers, open a terminal in the extracted folder and run node run-example.mjs.

Open Open my own recording in the viewer. Download one of the two examples to see the file format. For positions already in three dimensions, enter five frames with increasing times in seconds. Leave no more than 240 milliseconds between neighbouring frames. In each frame, give all twelve body points in the example's order. Enter each point as three coordinates in millimetres. Use null for each missing point. A joint needs its present reading and at least two earlier readings. The latest frame needs at least three visible body sites. The model keeps the last position when a visible joint has insufficient earlier support. An absent current point has no forecast.

For two cameras, the files must refer to the same person at matching times. Each camera needs numbers describing its lens, its position and its orientation. These are its calibration. Two simultaneous image locations give two lines of sight. Where the lines pass closest to one another gives a possible location in the room. A small angle between the lines makes depth uncertain. If a proposed point falls too far from the original images when projected back into them, the reader rejects it. Try the example camera pair shows this calculation with two illustrative cameras applied to a real Carnegie Mellon movement. No physical pair of cameras recorded that example.

A pair can use twelve named sites or all 33 points from MediaPipe. The earlier image model uses the available body points when estimating body size. Supplying all 33 reproduces the layout used in the recording study. With two cameras, Two-camera tracking feedback uses earlier resolved forecasts to decide how much to trust each image estimate. It gives a room-coordinate forecast when both cameras have issued one for the same joint and time. More history allows it to learn from more earlier errors.

Read the coordinates and run your own model

A position has three numbers. In a room calibration, they locate the point along the three axes of that room. If the coordinate for one axis changes from 120 to 145 mm, the change along that axis is 25 mm. The displayed movement also includes changes along the other two axes. Changing the drawing angle only changes your view. Changing the coordinate setting to Relative to hips changes what the numbers describe.

The two-camera calculation needs at least a two-degree angle between the lines of sight. It also checks that the reconstructed point projects within twelve pixels of both original readings. This check helps catch bad matches. It cannot correct a shared clock error or an incorrect calibration file. If you select image feedback, a point with no forecast at the same time in both views has no result for that time.

To change the model using your own recordings, open the model folder in the download. The Python program train.py reads one training example per line. Give it the earlier frames, the body point you want to predict, the measured position of that point 120 ms later and a group name identifying its participant. Put different people in the training and validation files. The validation recordings help you choose which fitted weights to keep. They do not supply the examples used for weight updates. The folder README gives the commands for fitting and then running the result.

For the original study, model/research contains the file lists and programs that read the source recordings and rebuild the training examples. We ran them a second time from the original files. All six sets of fitting and tuning examples matched the first run. The repeated training then produced the same 9,000-update weights, down to the last stored byte. On 1,861 separately checked joint forecasts, the portable JavaScript and Python results differed by at most 0.00033 mm on this computer. We also checked the two-camera method on 3,425 matched forecasts. Its largest difference from the original calculation was 0.000065 mm.

How the model learned

The model started with weights from the earlier human image model. For each joint, it looks at that joint, a neighbour, its partner on the opposite side and the average movement of visible body sites. It compares how far those sites moved and how closely their directions agree. Measuring motion relative to the spread of the visible points lets the same calculation work when a person or the units appear larger. The output is converted back to the original size and axes. Each of the five times can include eight right-leg muscle readings. From the first channel through the eighth, the right-leg signals come from the tibialis anterior, soleus, medial gastrocnemius, vastus medialis, rectus femoris, semitendinosus, gluteus maximus and gluteus medius. Each entry is a root mean square voltage over the preceding 30 milliseconds. Squaring the electrical readings, averaging them and taking the square root gives one number for the signal level in that short interval.

Two groups of attention calculations compare the five times. Each has four attention heads and 32 numbers to describe each time. Training adjusted 18,532 numbers, in addition to 120 fixed starting coefficients. The model saw 311,368 source training examples and a separate 73,634 examples used to choose when to stop. Of 9,500 updates, the version at 9,000 had the best tuning score. All compact-model training calculations used 32-bit floating point.

Carnegie Mellon contributed 3D movements covering the twelve sites. Its numbered source groups can include the same person. Georgia Tech contributed treadmill recordings with a left shoulder and both hips, knees and ankles. A third laboratory recorded overground walking at five speeds. We used 685 recordings from 30 people there for fitting and tuning. Another ten people supplied the overground test. Examples with artificially missing or displaced points taught the model to handle some tracking errors.

Two cameras and an independent measurement

The MoVi laboratory recorded people with two fixed cameras and a separate optical system. The optical system provided reference positions from reflective markers. The study used the published camera calibration to express all the positions in the same room. Position error measures how far the forecast ended from the later optical position. Movement error measures how far the forecast missed the change from the starting position. The tracker can start several centimetres off and still predict part of the movement correctly.

The cameras originally issued 293,462 forecasts at matching times for the same point. Of these, 288,620 had enough usable three-dimensional history. The camera estimate at the starting time was already 52.06 mm from the optical reference on average. At 202,251 matched joint times from 86 people, combining forecasts from the two images after tracking feedback gave the lowest average errors among the methods compared. Each person had equal weight in the final average. These people had been used for an earlier image study. This is a new three-dimensional comparison in that same cohort.

The earlier tracking-feedback method had 70.98 mm position error. The new direct 3D model had 73.13 mm. Its increase of 2.15 mm had a paired 95% uncertainty interval from 1.50 to 2.80 mm. For movement the increase was 3.85 mm, with an interval from 3.10 to 4.62 mm. These intervals come from repeatedly drawing whole participants from this cohort. The replacement rule also required every body point to stay within a 5 mm limit for worsening. The new direct model failed the replacement rule. The existing image-feedback route remains the first choice for calibrated paired tracks.

The larger recurrent Research Edition also had a separate, smaller test. On 108 matched camera-pair forecasts from 76 people, the compact starting model, the larger model with fixed internal weights, and the larger model after adapting selected weights had position errors of 84.48, 89.21 and 93.48 mm. Their movement errors were 49.49, 56.70 and 60.21 mm. The corrected downloadable version of that larger camera model gave 93.48 and 60.20 mm in a retrospective check.

Twelve points at once

The earlier camera methods issue each point on its own clock. Their original common forecast times contained no moment with all twelve points together. We went back to the synchronized camera readings and asked for all twelve whenever the paired history supported them. Of 396,536 recorded frame pairs, 5,404 allowed a complete forecast. Optical measurements for all twelve points supported scoring 5,138 of them, from 84 people. That is about 1.3% of the paired frames. A fully visible pose is a selected part of a recording.

On these complete poses, the trained 3D model reduced average room-position error by 13.36 mm compared with keeping the last position. The 95% interval for the reduction was 11.89 to 14.86 mm. Its movement error fell from 63.70 to 43.45 mm. In hip-centred coordinates, its position and movement errors were 110.95 and 47.60 mm. Keeping the last hip-centred positions gave 111.31 and 52.60 mm. Errors in either hip affect the coordinates of every other point.

Muscle measurements and one-camera depth

The walking recordings let us give the same model the same poses twice, once with muscle readings and once with them hidden. There were 25,064 clean optical comparisons. Supplying muscle readings increased mean error by 0.74 mm, with a paired 95% interval from 0.58 to 0.93 mm. All ten participants had higher error. In the 23,278 windows with artificially missing or noisy points, real muscle readings reduced average error by 2.40 mm. Each comparison used the same pose windows with the signal present and hidden. The clean and artificially degraded groups contained different sampled windows. That second row describes an artificial disturbance. The source used filtering across entire trials, which may draw on later readings. Neither result establishes the timing or accuracy of live muscle control.

Finally, we ran the game's own single-camera tracker on both camera recordings of each of two people. It estimates each point in three dimensions relative to the hips. Across 21,593 supported point comparisons, the trained model had 130.65 mm position error and 57.23 mm movement error. Keeping the last estimate gave 119.65 and 38.22 mm. We also compared how much the distance from the hips changed, a measure that ignores the direction of the camera axes. The trained model missed this change by 24.44 mm on average, compared with 13.18 mm for the last-position estimate. That result explains the default in the game panel. Depth accuracy on an individual home webcam still needs measurement.
