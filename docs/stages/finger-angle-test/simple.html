Predicting a finger’s next movement

Choose a participant, a movement or a finger in the interactive comparison. The number beside each method is how far its prediction missed, on average. A smaller number is better. The default view includes all 20 recordings from both test participants.

What the cameras and ultrasound measured

Six people took part in the original study. An ultrasound probe on the forearm recorded the echoes returning from tissue under the skin. Ten cameras tracked small markers on four fingers 100 times a second. The researchers used the markers to work out how much each finger bent at the knuckle where it meets the hand. The thumb had no markers. In the pinch movement, the participant touched the thumb with the index finger, but the score covers the four tracked fingers.

The researchers synchronized the two instruments. The analysis had one set of finger angles for each ultrasound image, 25 times a second. Eight frame intervals span 320 milliseconds, about a third of a second. The task was to predict the change in angle from the current instant to the end of that interval. To turn a predicted change into a future angle, you would also need the starting angle.

How the comparison worked

Models were fitted using 40 recordings from four people. The two test participants supplied ten recordings each. Each repeated an index-finger bend at medium speed, and a ring-finger bend, index-to-thumb pinch and hook at slow, medium and fast speeds. The movement was repeated to a beat. The methods had access to a history of 101 frames, spanning four seconds. The predictions for both people were saved before anyone used their later camera angles to score them.

One rule held each finger at its latest angle. Another looked for a similar stretch of movement earlier in the same recording. For each finger separately, it compared the most recent ten angles and their changes with earlier stretches ending eight to 75 frames back. It copied the angle reached eight frames after the closest match, then subtracted the current angle to predict the signed change. The comparison gave half as much weight to squared differences in the angle changes as to squared differences in the angles themselves. If two earlier stretches tied, the rule took the more recent one. The matched continuation had already happened. The adaptive rule chose between replay and holding the last angle using their recent completed errors. The trend rule extended a line through the latest five angles.

The six fitted methods used either finger angles, ultrasound, or both. The program divided each ultrasound image into a four-by-four grid. In each rectangle it restored the recorded echo amplitude using the file’s scale and averaged it. It added one and took the logarithm, which compresses large differences in echo strength. It then subtracted the same rectangle’s first compressed value in the current history. The scale used to put these inputs on a comparable footing for fitting came from the four fitting people. A linear model combined earlier readings with fitted weights. Its fitting penalty was 0.01, which restrained large weights. The recurrent model processed each history with 24 internal values and three passes. Each recurrent version was fitted twice, for 160 updates with 64 examples per update. The two forecasts were averaged.

Reading the errors

Suppose a finger straightens by 12 degrees and a method predicts 8 degrees of straightening. It misses by 4 degrees. Errors in either direction count positively. We averaged the four fingers within a recording, then the speeds within each movement, the movements within each person, and finally the two people. That gives the index bend the same weight as the hook, even though they were recorded at different numbers of speeds.

There were 26,000 forecast times. The last eight forecasts in each recording fell beyond its end, leaving 25,840 times with a measured answer for every method. Successive times come from the same people repeating a movement, so they do not count as 25,840 separate people.

For Participant A, replay missed by 5.38° and holding missed by 15.36°. For Participant B, the figures were 5.54° and 12.16°. Across the two people, replay reduced the average error from 13.76° to 5.46°, a reduction of 60.3%. The size of the gain depended on the movement. Select Make a hook to see errors of 3.21° for replay and 3.81° for holding. Select Bend the index finger to see 6.66° and 25.02°.

The ultrasound-only recurrent model missed by 16.01° overall. For these repeated gestures, the earlier camera angles supplied a better forecast. The two people used the same equipment and protocol as the four fitting participants. Performance on spontaneous gestures, other equipment, patients and live operation remains unknown.
