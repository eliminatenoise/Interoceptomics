Chest-vibration forecast methods

The selected ridge regression (R-ECG), a weighted linear formula fitted with a penalty on large weights, predicted the filtered chest accelerometer voltage 80 milliseconds (ms) ahead. Mean person-level root mean square error (RMSE) was 1.15640 millivolts (mV). A personal vibration template timed from the electrocardiogram (ECG) averaged 1.15704 mV. Its study label is T-ECG.

Forecast and test population

The source database recorded one axis of chest vibration (seismocardiography), a breathing band around the chest and the heart’s electrical activity (ECG). The study used the five-minute after-music recordings of people resting on their backs. It fitted on ten people, p004–p013, and scored the primary comparison once on seven others, p014–p020. Earlier participants and the separate baseline resting recording belong to secondary analyses and are outside this chart.

The instruments sampled at 5,000 Hz. The analysis suppressed fast fluctuations with a 65 Hz low-pass filter using only samples already observed, then kept 250 readings per second. Calibration used each person’s earlier readings from 60 to 80 seconds. At each cutoff from 90 seconds up to 290 seconds, the model predicted chest voltage 20 samples later. All primary participants supplied 50,000 valid cutoffs. Adjacent windows overlap.

R-ECG combined 51 earlier chest and 51 earlier breathing readings, four chest-timed beat features and 13 ECG-timed features. ECG lead II is one view of the heart’s electrical activity. Its R wave is a prominent spike from activation of the heart’s lower chambers. The preceding P wave comes from the upper chambers. The full personal template averages chest vibrations aligned on recent R waves and uses a matching P wave to anticipate the next R wave. Without a match it continues from the last R wave. The researchers selected and fixed the model family, template and ridge settings using only the ten fitting people.

The three prespecified decision rules passed. The first required at least a 10% mean reduction against refitted version 1 and lower error than the calibration mean on average and in at least six of the seven people. The second compared the ridge model with its no-ECG and 5.012-second delayed-ECG counterparts. The third compared it with C3, a personal template timed only from R waves detected by a separate detector. The protocol’s “beats” criterion combined a positive mean reduction, at least six wins among seven and a bootstrap lower bound above zero.

The full personal template

T-ECG uses the version 2 beat detector and anticipates beats from the P wave. It has no fitted regression weights. In the separately declared descriptive comparison, R-ECG beat T-ECG for five of seven people. The mean error reduction was +0.00064 mV (95% interval −0.00231 to +0.00293 mV), or +0.078% (−0.176% to +0.276%). The interval includes zero. A small numerical lead over this practical control does not establish a reliable advantage.

Calculating the chart

Within each person, RMSE is the square root of the mean squared difference between the predicted and recorded sensor voltage. The cohort bar is the arithmetic mean of the seven person-level RMSE values, each calculated from 50,000 forecasts. The 350,000 overlapping forecast cutoffs come from seven people. At each selection the chart displays one absolute error per method and a separate signed difference for each person, calculated as comparator RMSE minus version 2 RMSE. For p015, the full template’s 1.19689 mV minus the ridge model’s 1.20377 mV gave −0.00688 mV, rounded. The template had lower error. For p014 the difference was +0.00306 mV, favouring the fitted model.

The percentage reduction divides that signed difference by the comparator’s error for the same person, then multiplies by 100. The published 95% percentile intervals resample the seven people with replacement, keeping both scores from each person together. They retain the middle 95% of the resampled mean differences, using 10,000 draws and seed 20261127. They describe sampling variation in the seven-person mean. Six comparisons report both intervals directly against version 2. The study also reports the neural network (a fitted nonlinear model) against the ridge model in the opposite direction. For that selection the chart reverses the signed voltage difference and interval endpoints. All remaining chart differences are descriptive, without a published paired interval.

The outcome is millivolts at the sensor. Accuracy in another laboratory, during movement or on a live sensor requires new measurements. The recordings do not identify nerve signals, physical displacement or subjective experience.
