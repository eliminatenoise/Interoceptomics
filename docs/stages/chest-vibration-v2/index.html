The three recordings and the 80-millisecond forecast

Skin electrodes picked up changes in electrical activity as the heart beat. This recording is an electrocardiogram, or ECG. A band around the chest followed the rise and fall of breathing. An accelerometer attached to the chest picked up vibration in one direction. Its output voltage, in thousandths of a volt (millivolts, mV), is the quantity the methods tried to predict.

Each person lay on their back for five minutes after listening to music. The instruments took 5,000 readings a second. The researchers filtered out fast fluctuations with a 65 Hz low-pass filter, using only readings already taken. They kept 250 readings per second, one every four milliseconds. Readings from 60 to 80 seconds established each person’s usual level and variation. Every four milliseconds from 90 seconds until just before 290 seconds, the methods predicted the chest reading 20 samples, or 80 milliseconds, ahead.

Ten people, labelled p004 to p013, supplied the recordings used to choose the settings and fit the model. Those choices were fixed before testing. The seven people in the chart, p014 to p020, supplied different recordings from the same lab. Other recordings in the original study, including an earlier resting recording, have separate results.

How the heartbeat template predicts a voltage

In the ECG, a tall spike called the R wave marks electrical activation of the heart’s lower chambers. A smaller signal before it, the P wave, comes from the upper chambers. The study used a particular view of this electrical activity called lead II.

A method can line up the chest readings from recent beats so their R waves coincide. Averaging the vibrations at each point gives a typical beat for that person, called a template. If an earlier P wave looks familiar, the full template method estimates when the next R wave will occur. It can then read the expected voltage at the forecast time from the appropriate part of the template. Without a good P-wave match it continues from the last detected R wave.

The full template, labelled T-ECG, needs the person’s recent beats but has no forecast weights learned from other people. Version 2 adds a weighted formula, called ridge regression. Its 119 inputs include 51 earlier chest readings, 51 breathing readings, four measurements of beats timed from the chest sensor, and 13 measurements of beat timing and vibration derived using the ECG. During fitting, a penalty discourages excessively large weights.

One person’s errors and the direction of a difference

Select ECG-timed beat template and Person 015. Version 2’s error was 1.20377 mV. The template’s error was 1.19689 mV. For each forecast the researchers compared its predicted voltage with the later recorded voltage. They squared each miss, averaged the squares for that person, and took the square root to return to millivolts. This is root mean square error. Large misses carry more weight than small ones. Both bars start at zero.

The dot uses a different calculation. Take the template’s error and subtract version 2’s error. For p015 the result was −0.00688 mV, so the template was closer. For p014 the difference went the other way, +0.00306 mV, so version 2 was closer. The figures here are rounded to five decimal places. An error of 1.20377 mV summarizes predictions across one person’s test interval. You can see an individual forecast and its later sensor reading in the version 1 exercise.

Seven people, repeated forecasts and uncertainty

Each person supplied 50,000 forecasts. The next forecast came just four milliseconds after the last one and reused much of the same history. That makes 350,000 forecasts per method, all from seven people. To read the average bar, first find the error for each person as in the example above. Add the seven errors and divide by seven. Each person contributes equally to that average.

Counting wins answers a narrower question. Five people had smaller errors with version 2 and two with the full template. A large loss for one person can outweigh several small wins. Across the seven people, the average reduction in error was just +0.00064 mV. To express the improvement as a percentage, divide each person’s difference by that same person’s comparison error before averaging. That gave +0.078%.

To see how much the result depends on who was included, the study repeatedly drew seven people from the seven available. A person could appear twice or more in a draw. Their two methods’ scores always stayed together. The study made 10,000 such draws, using the same starting number, 20261127, so the calculation can be repeated. The middle 95% of averages ran from −0.00231 to +0.00293 mV, or −0.176% to +0.276% for the percentage calculation. Both intervals span zero. With these seven people, the study could not reliably tell which method would have the lower average error in another group like this one.

Comparisons the researchers chose before the test

Before opening the seven test recordings, the researchers wrote three conditions for success. Their usual rule for beating a method required a positive average percentage reduction, lower error for at least six of the seven people, and a 95% interval for the average percentage reduction entirely above zero.

  1. Version 2 had to beat the first model after it was fitted again on the ten fitting people, with at least a 10% average reduction. It also had to improve on always predicting the person’s calibration average, both on average and for at least six people. Against refitted version 1 the reduction was 50.78% (95% interval 47.32% to 54.71%).
  2. Version 2 had to beat two versions of the same formula. One used chest and breathing without ECG. The other supplied ECG timing from 5.012 seconds earlier. The reductions were 46.92% against each after rounding. The intervals were 42.46% to 51.36% and 42.45% to 51.36%, respectively.
  3. Version 2 had to beat a simpler personal template labelled C3. It used a separate detector and timed beats from R waves alone. The reduction was 40.29% (33.97% to 46.19%).

All three conditions passed. Each of the four comparisons with an interval above favoured version 2 for all seven people. The almost identical errors you see when the chart first opens belong to a separate comparison with the full personal template, which can also use the earlier P wave. That comparison describes how much the fitted formula added to the template. It was outside the three success conditions.

The study published paired intervals comparing version 2 directly with six methods. It also compared a neural network, a fitted formula that can learn curved relationships, with version 2 in the opposite order. The study subtracted the network’s error from version 2’s. This chart subtracts version 2’s error from the network’s, as it does for each other method. Both signs reverse and the two ends of the voltage interval exchange places. For other pairs without a published interval, you can still read the size and direction of the measured difference.

What these measurements cover and where they came from

The result concerns chest-sensor voltage at rest after music. There were no measurements of how strongly these people felt a heartbeat or of what a nerve was doing. The study did not convert sensor voltage to a distance moved. Forecasting during exercise, in another laboratory or from a sensor operating live would require a separate test.
