Find a recording

Open Human movement. The first step has a recording of someone steering a cursor with their right wrist. For a rat running on a track, open Animal learning · Navigation. You can start with either recording. For another step, first choose a group in Stage or task, then select a name from the list below it. The guide has 50 steps arranged in 14 groups. Previous step and Next step move one step at a time. The left and right arrow keys do the same when you are outside a slider or menu.

Some steps have several viewers. Under Explore this step, choose one in Model to explore. Read the description and choose Open here when that button appears. Close model takes you back to the control that opened it. To give a viewer its own window, use its separate-tab link. NeuroSurge opens in a separate tab.

To send someone your view, choose Copy link to this view under the step title. Set Task time and View angle first. The link also keeps Limb and Reference where the step offers them.

In the right-wrist trial, the guide checks the recording files first. The link keeps the time shown on the traces and the choices you made under Last input, after the cue and Measurements the model used. If the file check fails, choose Try again in the wrist recording before copying the view.

A selected companion stays selected, and the recipient chooses when to open it. Changes made inside a companion viewer stay in that viewer. If the browser cannot copy the link, it puts the address in a field so you can copy it yourself.

Try movement tracking in the game

With internet access, open Enter a virtual challenge. Under Explore this step, choose Open NeuroSurge in a separate tab. Choose Full or Lite. Both editions have the same movement controls. Lite downloads smaller anatomy files.

Press C to open the motion recorder. To use your camera, choose Start camera and allow video access. Use Follow to choose which limbs the avatar copies. For a video, choose Recorded video, open a Local video file and select Play video. For Timed pictures, choose your pictures. Enter one time in seconds for each picture in the order the file list shows. Start at zero or later and give each picture a different time. Choose Track pictures.

Choose Movement forecast in the toolbar or press F and enter “forecast a tracked point”. This closes the recorder and opens a separate view. Select Source again. Choose Start camera for your camera or open a Local video file and choose Play selected clip. For Timestamped stills, open the images and enter the times in the displayed file order. Use seconds, with no negative or repeated times. Choose Track keyframes. Body follows selects which part of the virtual body will copy the movement. Under Point to forecast, you can choose another point. The preview uses five images with no more than 0.24 seconds between consecutive images. The point must be visible in the last image and at least two earlier ones. The forecast shows where that point may appear 120 milliseconds later, a little over a tenth of a second. The stay-put prediction uses the last position as its forecast. Both predictions are scored against the same later position, measured within 0.04 seconds of the target time. Both errors are in pixels, the small units that make up a picture.

Press F to open the Command box and type a supported action such as “lift my right wrist above my shoulder”. You can speak a command if your browser offers on-device speech input. In the forecast view, choose Save take once the virtual body follows the limb to keep up to eight seconds of that movement in this tab. Select Open Command box and enter “replay my take” to repeat it.

The game uses its input to move a virtual body. The wrist study below uses measurements from a separate recording. The forecast accuracy tests for the game used recorded pictures. Read the forecast test.

Follow the wrist from the cue to the later reading

In From brain signals to movement · Brain2Body, the person saw a target and moved the cursor towards it. Move Time from the visual cue. You can look at half a second before the target appeared and the two and a half seconds that followed. The dashed line marks the cue. The moving line marks the instant you are reading.

Look at what each instrument could measure. An electrode at a position called C3 picked up voltage at the scalp. Two electrodes on the forearm picked up voltage near muscles. Their trace shows the difference between the two readings. A microvolt is one millionth of a volt, a useful unit for these small signals. The wrist device had two angle sensors. One measured the bend of the hand and the other measured how far it tilted sideways. Read these angles in degrees.

Now choose Last input, after the cue. This sets the last instant the model could use. Under Measurements the model used, start with Wrist angles, then try Wrist angles + forearm. Compare the saved guesses for the bend and tilt a quarter of a second later. Show this outcome on the traces takes you to the instant the wrist sensors measured those angles.

A model can look accurate on one movement and do poorly on others. This demonstration was left out of a separate comparison of 959 trials from three people. The study combined misses on both wrist angles and gave each person equal weight. It squared each miss, averaged those squared values and took the square root to return to degrees. Squaring gives larger misses more weight. This error rose from 5.432 to 5.866 degrees when the model used the forearm readings. Who recorded the wrist and how the forecasts were made.

Compare hand forecasts with recent motion

Open Compare hand forecasts from motion and brain activity and choose Open here. The same reader is under Mechanics and From brain signals to movement. It compares six ways of guessing where a hand will be a tenth of a second later. The results cover 130,723 forecasts from 9,075 reaching trials on three previously evaluated days from one macaque.

Choose a Recording day, then start with All recorded forecasts. One forecast extends the speed and direction measured over the previous 40 milliseconds. The equal blend predicts a point halfway between that forecast and one that used brain activity. Compared with extending recent motion alone, the blend reduced the average error at the ending point by 1.07, 0.30 and 1.74 mm on the three days. On 19 August, its error in how far the hand moved was 0.08 mm higher on average. If a hand goes 10 mm right and the forecast goes 10 mm left, the distance is right and the ending point is 20 mm off.

Use Error to compare to switch between the two measures. Under Movements to show, compare all reaches with movements of different sizes. Groups marked “afterwards” use the measured result. In the coordinate editor, change the earlier hand positions and the example neural forecast. The starting numbers are invented. Editing them leaves the recorded table as it was. The reader and both editions work in the downloaded Stage Guide. Read the hand forecast methods · Open the earlier four-forecast comparison.

Finger angles during a pinch or hook

In Large Movement Models, choose Pinch and hook forecasts, then select Open here. In the extracted Stage Guide, the forecaster and its Simple guide and Technical guide open without an internet connection. The same reader is available on the NeuroTranslate website.

Start with the invented index pinch. Choose a finger and compare its angle now with the forecast 320 milliseconds later. Both angles describe how far the finger bends where it meets the hand. Choose Replay an earlier movement to see which part of the last four seconds resembled the latest movement. Select Include ultrasound readings to give the fitted models the example readings from sixteen parts of an ultrasound image. Those starting values are invented too.

Under Forecast errors on recorded movements, replay missed by 4.03 degrees on average across four fingers and four people. Holding the last angle missed by 6.70 degrees. Each person counted equally in those averages. The study ran eight separate tests. For each test, it fitted several models on the other people and other gestures, then scored the person and gesture set aside. The fitted models you can try in the browser learned separately from all 40 development recordings. These models forecast finger angles and are separate from NeuroTranslate 0.0 and 0.1.

The downloaded Stage Guide also includes the saved fitted models and a Python program. The reader’s Technical guide gives the commands. If the listed Python packages are already installed, the program can run without internet too.

Forecast changes inside two arm muscles

At Movement model, select Predict change inside two arm muscles and choose Open here. The same forecast is available under Mechanics, From brain signals to movement and Myoception. This human example forecasts a change in the spacing of points tracked in an earlier ultrasound study. The study's measurements show how the forecasts scored. Measuring what a person senses through their muscles requires different evidence.

The model starts with an invented example. It estimates how much the spacing of tracked points in ultrasound images of the triceps and brachialis would change over the next 300 milliseconds. A number below zero means the points are expected to draw closer together. A number above zero means they are expected to spread out. Compare the forecasts that use earlier arm positions with those that also use voltages recorded near the arm muscles and palm. You can open a compatible local file or restore the example. The guide runs the calculations on your device with the camera off.

The second table compares the five methods on 7,503 recorded times for each muscle. The six people came from one reaching study and were set aside before training. The linear forecast using positions alone averaged 0.153 mm of error across the two muscles. The uncertainty range for its advantage over predicting no change includes a tie for each muscle. Testing with a live instrument would require a separate check of when each reading becomes available. The reader also works from the downloaded Stage Guide. Reaching study and downloads

Compare hand forecasts with measured force

Open Compare hand forecasts with measured force and choose Open here. This comparison comes from one recorded macaque session. Choose a group based on how far the hand moved or whether the handle delivered a push during the trial. Switch between ending position and distance error. Positive changes mean the extra reading lowered the error.

On the same 624 forecasts from 74 later trials, adding measured force lowered average ending position error from 2.396 to 2.276 mm. Adding sensory cortex raised the overall error to 2.328 mm. Choose At least 20 mm to see where the extra brain readings helped. The downloaded Stage Guide includes the same comparison.

Read a body-worn sensor, then a floor plate

Under Human movement, choose Open the walking recording. In Wearable sensor, pick an arm, a leg or the torso. The person wore five sensors while walking through a parking lot. Each measured acceleration along three directions fixed to that sensor. Speeding up, slowing down and tilting relative to gravity can all change its readings.

Use Forecast to compare. One option repeats the last reading. You can compare it with a model trained on all five body parts. The answer to compare them with is the sensor reading 120 milliseconds later, a little over a tenth of a second. This walk helped train the model. In the separate 3D drawing, select a Limb, move Task time and turn View angle. The limb follows a programmed movement. The recorded sensor values have their own control. Walking sensor recording and credits.

For force, open Walking forecasts with measured force, then Open here. A different study followed 16 people with an above-knee prosthesis walking at 0.6 metres per second. Cameras tracked markers on their heels. Plates in the floor measured how hard each foot pressed down. The model used the preceding fifth of a second to predict the next tenth of a second. A newton measures force. Adding up force over the time it acts gives an impulse in newton-seconds. Each walker's impulse forecast improved when the model could use the floor readings as well as the heels. The average miss fell from 16.35 to 7.13 newton-seconds. The separate downloadable model lets you try made-up starting values.

For NeuroTranslate 0.1, open Compare walking forecasts by pass count and choose Open here. Switch between force and new-load probability to compare five settings of the same model on nine recorded walks.

Standing and sitting force forecast uses another study. Open the reader to change an invented history from the two floor plates and compare three forecasts. The study set three people's recordings aside for testing. Two passed the force check and supplied 230 short intervals in which to check a prediction. A floor plate measures load from outside the body. To find out how a step or a chair movement felt, someone would have to ask the person.

Open Forecast force and waist rotation and choose Open here. Change the earlier force plate or waist watch values and watch four predictions for the next tenth of a second. The edited examples use invented readings. Choose Results on recorded data to compare the methods on people’s recordings. This exercise also runs from the downloaded Stage Guide.

Open Compare seven force forecasts and choose Open here. Change ten earlier readings from one floor plate and compare the predicted push over the next tenth of a second. In the recorded results, switch between a plate that was already carrying load and one that carried very little. Read the floor force guide.

Open Forecast insole pressure to try a different measurement. An insole has pressure pads at places beneath each foot. Edit one generated reading and compare estimates for a tenth of a second later. A pad reports pressure at its own location. A floor plate measures the whole push on the plate. Read the insole guide.

Find the rat’s later position

Open 0.2 · Navigation. Choose a Training trip. Five dots on the map show where the camera saw the rat during the preceding second. The largest dot marks the last position the models could use. Under Prediction to mark on the maps, start with Past positions. A diamond marks the estimated position a quarter of a second later. Switch to Past positions + spikes. A spike is a brief electrical impulse. The researchers counted impulses from recorded brain cells in four successive quarter-seconds. The second model also used those earlier counts.

Choose Reveal the later camera reading. The model aims for a quarter of a second after its last input. The camera may have taken no reading at that exact instant. The plus sign marks the first stored position at or after the requested time, up to 50 milliseconds later. The table tells you how far each prediction missed that position in centimetres. The models learned how much weight to give each input from 24 trips. The fitting method, called ridge regression, keeps the weights from growing too large. The three trips you can select are among them. Across nine later trips, counting each trip equally, the average miss was 2.01 centimetres using positions and 2.24 centimetres when the earlier spikes were added.

Further down, Camera frame moves through another run of the rat in the same published study. This replay shows the recorded spike times for two units. The recording analysis grouped spikes attributed to the same neuron into a unit. Below it, the straight corridor illustrates a different experiment someone has proposed. Its moving point follows the software’s instructions. Turning the corridor or changing Task time cannot change where the camera saw the real rat. Forecast study · Camera replay and sources.

Follow the muscle, organ and walking examples

Choose Animal learning. Under Stage or task, choose Back to human function. Myoception is the programme’s term for sensing the movements your muscles make and the feedback you receive as you move. This includes feeling where a part of your body is and, when it matters to the movement, what it touches. Choose Trace a signal from a muscle, then Open here. The atlas shows structures along a possible route into the spinal cord and brain. Close the atlas and choose Count the spikes from a muscle spindle. A muscle spindle senses stretch. An apparatus stretched a rat's calf while an electrode recorded impulses in one sensory nerve fibre. The nerve signal was sampled 20,000 times each second. The apparatus position and tendon force were sampled 2,000 times each second.

There is also a human walking example that compares how often leg muscles and knees repeat a pattern. Its instruments had separate time windows. You can compare their rhythms, much as you can count how often two signals rise in a minute. The recordings could not establish which particular burst of muscle activity went with a particular bend of the knee.

In the same group, choose Visceroception, sensing conditions inside organs. The atlas traces sensory pathways, including signals from stretch. Then select Read the heartbeat and breathing traces and Open here. In this separate study the person was at rest. The ECG shows the heart’s electrical activity, the chest sensor picks up vibration and a band responds to breathing. One cursor shows all three readings at the same instant. Move the cursor to another beat and read the three signals again. These are instrument readings. The person’s own report would be needed to say what they noticed.

Compare chest-vibration forecasts at rest. Choose Open here, then choose a person and a method. The three-signal model forecast the chest sensor’s voltage 80 ms ahead. It had lower error than the personal heartbeat template for five of seven test people, with almost identical average errors. Version 1 lets you reveal a recorded voltage.

Both sensory steps offer Follow the exercise and pressure loop. The atlas identifies structures on the routes between working muscles, the brainstem, the heart and blood vessels. Its pictures come from different anatomical specimens. They show where structures lie. Backslash atlas simple edition.

To compare measurements during walking, open Neural Composite · Follow one stride from muscles to the floor. A stride runs from one footfall to the next landing of the same foot. Follow the group’s average slow stride. Cameras tracked 52 markers on the skin, electrodes picked up voltage near eight muscles in the right leg, and two floor plates measured load. The equipment recorded the same walks. The curves line up by percentage of a stride, so halfway means halfway through that cycle. Different curves may average different walkers. A full neural composite would also trace brain and spinal cord signals during that action. Those signals were missing in these walks. The menu entry Compare firing and movement on a recorded trip opens a rat study on a separate clock.

Compare zebrafish tail forecasts. Choose a minute from one zebrafish recording. Compare four guesses with the amount of tail-angle variation the camera measured in the next tenth of a second. Select this companion in MotorSensation.

Compare tail forecasts across 12 recordings. Compare seven rules for guessing how much a zebrafish tail angle will vary. Select each of 12 later recordings to see where commands helped and where another rule was closer. Select the 12-recording companion in MotorSensation.

Explore what the proposed tasks would test

Under Human movement, the groups cover mapping a movement, using the hands, coordinating several limbs, and responding at the right time. Under Animal learning, five introductory aims lead into 22 numbered steps across Stages 0–5. Stage 0 describes navigation, orientation and replay. Stage 1 would test whether a specified human-related feature in a model organism changes a measurable function. Stage 2 concerns donor tissue. Seeing tissue in the host would establish its presence. A separate measurement would have to show what it contributes to the host. Stage 3 considers whether one animal could learn from another. Stage 4 considers guidance from software. Stage 5 would require a defined difficulty, a task and a way to measure change. At each step, Evidence and limits explains what would have to be measured. The link below it opens the relevant page of the technical script.

On a replay step, look for Reference. Choose Matched and move Task time. Then choose Delayed and watch the reference fall behind. Off removes it. This drawing shows how guidance could change. To find out whether a learner retained a skill, the experiment would have to remove the guidance, measure what the learner could still do and test again later. Comparable practice without that guidance would help explain the result. The stage runs a drawing on your computer. The proposed interventions and transfer of skill have no measured result here, and the stage has no connection to a live device.

Open the guide without an internet connection

While online, open the experiment guide and choose Download the guide for offline use (ZIP). A ZIP stores many files together. Extract the entire folder first so the browser can find the recordings, pictures and pages beside one another.

Install Python 3.10 or newer if you need it. You also need a current browser. In the extracted folder, open Windows.cmd on Windows, macOS.command on a Mac or Linux.sh on Linux. Leave the launcher window open while reading. The launcher serves the files on your own computer. If the browser does not open automatically, copy the local address printed in the launcher into your browser. Press Ctrl+C in the launcher window when you have finished.

The package includes the stage recordings, the Backslash atlas, other bundled readers and the two script PDFs. Copy link to this view gives you a public address. Someone else can use it after you stop your launcher. External NeuroTranslate studies, the NeuroSurge game, separate downloads and original publications need an internet connection.
