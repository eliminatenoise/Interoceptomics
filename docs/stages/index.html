How do we study movement?

Follow one right wrist

A participant used their right wrist to steer a cursor towards a target. Electrodes picked up tiny voltage changes at the scalp and forearm. Two sensors on the wrist device measured the bend of the hand and its tilt from side to side.

The recording

Each trace runs from half a second before the cue to 2.5 seconds after. The dashed line marks the cue. The solid line marks your reading.

Both wrist traces use the same scale. Positive angles mean the wrist bends back or tilts towards the thumb. Each voltage trace has its own scale.

What happened a quarter-second later?

Choose the last instant the model could see. It used the preceding half-second and the time since the cue to forecast both angles.

Wrist angleBendSide tilt

These forecasts were calculated in advance with the three trained NeuroTranslation models. This public example was excluded from the 959-trial comparison. Across those trials from three people, adding the forearm signals increased the model’s error from 5.432° to 5.866°.

What did the instruments record?

The displayed scalp voltage comes from electrode C3. The forearm trace subtracts the reading at M3R from the reading at M3L. Both use microvolts (µV). Every 16 stored rows, the full model calculates an average for each of eight scalp channels and a voltage amplitude for each of four forearm pairs.

The file stores 512 rows per second. The angle sensors update at 100 readings per second, so neighbouring rows can repeat the same angle. Hardware delays are unknown. The target marker records when the screen cued the movement. Spinal activity, muscle force and sensory nerve feedback would each require a separate measurement.

Jorge German Perez Blanco and Javier Mauricio Anteliz Ortiz, EEG-EMG-WristMotion Dataset, version 3. Dataset · CC BY 4.0 · Trial and forecast sources · Run the predictor ↗

Follow a rat across a W-shaped track

A camera tracked one male Long–Evans rat while electrodes picked up the brief electrical signals that brain cells produce when they fire. Each event is called a spike. The study marked this trip as a run from the centre well to the left well. Move the slider to compare a camera reading with the times two of the recorded cells fired.

Where was the rat?

The pale dots are the camera readings from the whole trip. The orange ring marks your frame. Both axes use centimetres in the study’s track coordinates.

When did the cells fire?

Each short stroke marks a spike from one cell. Both rows cover the same 48.899 seconds. The orange line follows your camera frame.

How close are the two measurements in time?

The camera positions and spikes share a session clock. The file holds 1,387 tracked positions, with 20–91 milliseconds between readings. The rat’s exact position between those readings is unknown. The source names the centre well as its reference frame and gives no compass bearing.

The study sorted the electrical events into groups attributed to individual cells. This excerpt takes the first listed unit in each of two brain regions. Their timing alone cannot tell us which cell made the rat turn. Testing feedback would require measuring what guidance reached an animal and how it responded.

Justin Shin and Shantanu Jadhav, Single Day W-Track Learning (2024). Dataset · CC BY 4.0 · Trial, units and source files

Read a real walking sensor

A walker wore sensors on both arms, both legs and the torso. As a leg speeds up, slows down or tilts relative to gravity, its sensor reading changes. This is the acceleration the model tries to predict. Pick one and see how close the computer came to its reading 120 milliseconds later. The computer learned from this parking-lot walk.

AccelerationXYZ

The first 16 readings span 0 to 600 milliseconds (ms). Compare the prediction with the reading at 720 ms. X, Y and Z are directions on the sensor, and the numbers use its original units. The recording measures acceleration. Joint angles and nerve activity would each need a different measurement.
