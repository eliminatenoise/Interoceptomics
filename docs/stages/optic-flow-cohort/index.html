The angle a camera could measure

Ryosuke Tanaka and Ruben Portugues filmed larval zebrafish while a visual pattern moved. The camera measured a bend angle. A positive angle marks a bend in one direction and a negative angle a bend in the other. The unit is the radian. A tail that remains bent to one side can have a large angle and very little variation. A tail moving between the two sides can vary considerably even if its average angle is close to zero.

The quantity the models predicted

A forecast was considered every 320 milliseconds on the recording’s software clock. Each rule estimated how widely the tail angles would spread in the next tenth of a second. Begin with the first angle at or after the forecast time and stop before the far end of that interval. To calculate the measured spread, find the average angle, square each angle’s distance from that average, average those squares and take the square root. This quantity is called the population standard deviation. The size of the forecast miss is the distance between the estimated spread and the spread the camera actually measured, in radians.

The visual direction in the log

A program chose when to change the direction of the moving pattern. This is called open loop because the fish’s swimming did not determine the next command. A command is a record of what the computer requested. The software timestamp cannot tell us exactly when the screen showed that direction, when the camera was exposed or when a live system could receive the reading. No neural activity was measured for this comparison.

Read the seven rows

The first rule carries the most recent spread forward. It starts with all the tail angles from the preceding tenth of a second and assumes that the next tenth will vary by the same amount. There are also three linear models and three Transformers. The linear models learn a correction to the first rule. They take the average angle and its spread from each of four earlier 50-millisecond intervals. A Transformer is a model that learns how much attention to give one reading in light of the others. All seven rules produce a spread of zero or greater.

Separate camera history from command history

In each model family, start with the row that uses only the camera. The current-command row also uses ten direction readings spread over the last five seconds, up to the forecast time. In the older-command row, every one of those ten times moves another six seconds back. Those older directions came from the same recording and may still be informative. Comparing the current and older rows tests whether the timing of the command history mattered for the forecast.

Why the average gives each recording one vote

The study used 29 whole recordings. Thirteen recordings from 13 April 2023 were used to fit the first models. Four other April recordings were used to check a model choice, the Transformer’s width, or number of features in its internal representation. The check selected width 24. Repeating the recent movement had the smallest average miss on those four recordings, 0.023542 radians. The final models were fitted using all 17 April recordings. Each recording had the same total weight.

Read the later comparison

On 16 May 2023 there were 12 test recordings. The models issued forecasts for 90,227 windows. The later camera angles could not supply a usable spread in 78 of them. Each of the remaining 90,149 windows could be scored for every rule. First average the misses in one recording. Then average those 12 recording scores. A recording with twice as many windows still gets one vote. Select a recording to see its own issued and scored counts.

Two comparisons with nine wins

The Transformer using the camera and current commands gave the smallest overall miss, 0.020223 radians. The camera-only Transformer missed by 0.020686 radians. Subtracting gives an improvement of 0.000463 radians from adding the commands. Repeating the recent camera spread missed by 0.023516 radians. The current-command Transformer improved on that rule by 0.003293 radians. This larger figure compares the whole model with the simple repeat rule. It includes information the model already had from the camera.

The recordings behind the exceptions

Against repeating recent movement, the current-command Transformer did better in nine recordings. The repeat rule was closer in f1, f4 and f8. Against the camera-only Transformer, the current-command Transformer also did better in nine. Camera alone was closer in f2, f4 and f11. There were no ties. Choose f8 to see how a command can improve the Transformer’s camera forecast while the repeat rule still has the smallest miss of those three.

Where the result applies

The short names f1 to f12 identify May recordings. Some numbers were reused in April. May f7 and f8 are different files carrying the same session label. This makes it difficult to tell how many individual animals or independent sessions the two dates represent. The scores describe these recordings. They give neither a range of likely errors for new animals nor evidence that changing the screen caused a particular bend. Another laboratory and equipment with verified physical timing would provide separate tests.

Ryosuke Tanaka and Ruben Portugues recorded the source data for Algorithmic dissection of optic flow memory in larval zebrafish. These forecasts and error summaries were calculated from the Figure 4 recordings.
